01 Overview

The spacecraft is the smallest part of it

A space mission is a spacecraft, a launch vehicle, a trajectory, a communications network and a team of people who will spend the next twenty years reading its telemetry. The hardware that leaves the ground is the part that gets photographed and very nearly the least of the problem.

Everything about mission design comes back to one quantity. Delta-v is the total change in velocity a spacecraft can manage with the propellant it carries, and it is the currency the whole discipline is denominated in. You do not travel a distance in space; you buy a velocity change, and the price is exponential in the mass you are moving.

Two consequences follow, and between them they explain most of what this page describes. The first is that departures are not optional in their timing: a transfer to Mars is only cheap when the two planets are correctly arranged, which happens about every 26 months, and a mission that misses its window waits. The second is that if you cannot afford the velocity you need, you borrow it. A gravity assist steals a fraction of a planet's orbital momentum, changing the spacecraft's speed relative to the Sun for no propellant at all — and the planet loses exactly as much, spread over a body 1020 times heavier, which is why nobody minds.

Then the long part begins. Cruise phases run for years, and a spacecraft on the far side of the Solar System is a few watts of transmitter answering a question that was asked two days ago. The Deep Space Network's three complexes — California, Spain and Australia, spaced roughly 120 degrees apart so something is always facing outward — are the reason any of it comes back at all.

Nobody has ever flown to another planet. Every mission fell there, accurately.

Six kinds of mission, in order of difficulty
  • Flyby. Pass once, take everything you can. Cheapest, and it is how every outer planet was first seen properly.
  • Orbiter. Arrive and then brake, which costs delta-v you had to carry all the way. Buys years of coverage instead of hours.
  • Lander. Arrive and then stop completely. On a world with an atmosphere this is mostly a heat problem; on one without, entirely a propellant problem.
  • Rover. A lander that then has to survive, navigate and decide things with a signal delay measured in minutes.
  • Sample return. Everything above, then a launch from the surface of somewhere else, then a rendezvous. Achieved from the Moon, from asteroids and from comet dust.
  • Crewed. All of it, with a mass budget for air, water, food and shielding, and no acceptable failure modes.

02 Apollo & the Moon

Twelve people walked there, and then nobody did

Six Apollo missions landed between July 1969 and December 1972. They returned 382 kilograms of rock that rewrote lunar geology, left instruments that are still being used, and then stopped — not because the problem was solved but because the programme was cancelled. In April 2026 a crew went back as far as lunar orbit for the first time since.

02a What it left

Instruments that are still reporting

Apollo 11, 14 and 15 each left a retroreflector array — a passive panel of corner-cube prisms that returns a laser pulse straight back to its source. Observatories have been bouncing lasers off them for over fifty years, which is how we know the Moon is receding from Earth at 3.8 centimetres a year. No power, no maintenance, no failure mode. They are the longest-running experiment in planetary science and they will outlast everything else on this page.

02b The return

Artemis II, April 2026

Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover and Christina Koch of NASA, with Jeremy Hansen of the Canadian Space Agency, flew a free-return loop around the Moon between 1 and 11 April 2026 and splashed down in the Pacific. It was the first crewed flight beyond low Earth orbit since Apollo 17 in December 1972, and at its farthest point it took them further from Earth than anyone has been. No landing — that is Artemis IV's job, now scheduled for 2028.

03 Voyager

Two probes, four planets, and an alignment that will not repeat

In the late 1970s Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune were arranged so that a single spacecraft could chain gravity assists from one to the next and reach all four on a budget that would otherwise have got it to Jupiter and stopped. The arrangement recurs roughly every 175 years. Voyager 2 took it, and remains the only spacecraft to have visited Uranus or Neptune.

Both spacecraft are powered by the decay of plutonium-238, and both are running out. The generators lose roughly four watts a year, so instruments have been switched off one at a time for decades to keep the rest alive. Voyager 1's round-trip signal time is now about two days: a command sent on Monday gets its acknowledgement on Wednesday, at a received power of around a billion-billionth of a watt.

They will keep going after the transmitters stop. Neither is aimed at anything — interstellar space is empty enough that aiming is meaningless — and both carry a gold-plated phonograph record of sounds and images from Earth, on the reasonable assumption that nothing will ever play it.

Why Voyager has not left the Solar System
  • It left the heliosphere, which is the bubble of particles and magnetic field the Sun blows into the interstellar medium. That boundary is the heliopause, and crossing it in 2012 is what "entered interstellar space" means.
  • The Sun's gravity reaches much further. The Oort Cloud — still bound, still orbiting — is thought to extend to something like 100,000 AU. Voyager 1 at 174 AU is roughly two thousandths of the way to its outer edge.
  • On that definition it has about 30,000 years to go. Both statements are true at once, which is why the headlines and the mission scientists appeared to disagree in 2013.

04 James Webb

A telescope that had to unfold itself correctly

Webb's mirror is too large to fit inside any rocket fairing ever built, so it launched folded and opened itself over two weeks, a million and a half kilometres from the nearest engineer. The deployment had 344 single-point failures — steps with no backup, where one jam ends a twenty-five-year programme. All of them worked.

04a The cold side

A sunshield the size of a tennis court

An infrared telescope has to be colder than the things it is looking at, or it photographs itself. Webb's five-layer kite-shaped shield keeps the telescope side at around 40 kelvin while the Sun side sits above 350 — a temperature difference of more than 300 degrees across a few metres of vacuum and aluminised film. That is also why it sits at the second Lagrange point: from there the Sun, Earth and Moon all stay in the same direction, so one shield covers all three.

04b Why infrared

The first galaxies are red now

Light from the earliest galaxies left them as ultraviolet and visible, and the expansion of the universe has stretched it by a factor of ten or more on the way here. It arrives as infrared, which the atmosphere absorbs and Hubble was never built to see. That single fact is the mission's justification: not a better telescope, a telescope pointed at a part of the spectrum where the objects we most wanted were hiding. The same wavelengths also see straight through the dust that hides star formation, which is why the Nebulae region looks the way it does now.

05 Mars

The atmosphere is thin enough to be a problem and thick enough to be one

Mars has about one per cent of Earth's surface pressure. That is more than enough to destroy a spacecraft arriving at 20,000 kilometres an hour, and nowhere near enough to stop one with parachutes. Every successful landing has used a hybrid of heat shield, chute and either retro-rockets or airbags, executed autonomously, because the signal takes minutes each way.

Landing is where Mars missions fail. Roughly half of everything ever sent has been lost, and the losses cluster in the seven minutes between the top of the atmosphere and the ground — a stretch that has to run entirely on software, because at Mars distance a radio round trip takes between eight and forty minutes and the whole descent is over before anyone on Earth knows it started.

What arrives is worth it. Orbiters have mapped the surface to better resolution than much of Earth's seafloor, found the seasonal frosts, tracked the dust storms and traced the ancient shorelines. The rovers have confirmed what the orbiters implied: liquid water stood on this surface for long enough to lay down clay and sulphate beds, in conditions a terrestrial microbe would have found unremarkable.

The orbiters do the unglamorous half
  • They are the relay. A rover's own antenna is small and its power budget is tight. Most surface data reaches Earth by being handed to an orbiter overhead, which then transmits it with a bigger dish and more watts.
  • They pick the landing sites. Every rover destination on this page was chosen from orbital imagery, years in advance, by arguing about rocks at metre resolution.
  • Some have been there a very long time. Mars Odyssey arrived in 2001 and is still working, which makes it the longest continuously operating spacecraft at another planet.

06 What's next

Six things that are funded, built or flying

Every item below is hardware that exists or is under construction, with a date attached. Nothing on this list is a concept study, and the two that have already launched are simply in transit — which is the ordinary condition of a deep-space mission for most of its life.

The pattern worth noticing is the lead time. Europa Clipper left Earth in October 2024 and does not arrive until 2030; Dragonfly launches in 2028 and reaches Titan in 2034. A mission's cruise is usually longer than its science phase, and the people who will operate it are frequently not the people who designed it.

Everything here is drawn from this region's cyan half — planned rather than flown. Dates on deep-space missions move, and the honest thing to say about any of them is that the arrival year is a good estimate and the launch year is a better one.

  1. 2027 · Artemis III Rehearsal in Earth orbit Crew practise the rendezvous and docking with a commercial landing system that Artemis IV depends on. Reassigned from a landing after the programme's architecture was revised.
  2. 2028 · Artemis IV First crewed South Pole landing The first people on the lunar surface since 1972, and the first anywhere near a pole — which is where the permanently shadowed water ice is.
  3. 2028 · Dragonfly launch A rotorcraft for Titan Nuclear-powered, eight rotors, flying between sample sites in an atmosphere four times denser than Earth's at one seventh the gravity. Arrives 2034.
  4. 2030 · Europa Clipper arrives Forty-nine flybys of an ocean moon Launched October 2024. It will not orbit Europa — the radiation would kill it — so it orbits Jupiter instead and passes close roughly fifty times.
  5. 2031 · JUICE arrives Into orbit around Ganymede ESA's mission, launched April 2023. It will be the first spacecraft to orbit a moon other than our own.
  6. Under review · Mars Sample Return Perseverance's tubes, still on Mars The samples are cached and waiting. The architecture to collect them has been redesigned repeatedly on cost grounds and currently has no confirmed date.

Misconceptions

Six things that sound right and are not

Rockets work by pushing against the air.

They push against their own exhaust. A rocket throws mass backwards and is thrown forwards by the same reaction, which needs nothing outside the vehicle at all — and works measurably better in vacuum, because there is no atmospheric pressure at the nozzle exit fighting the expanding gas. This was argued about in print as late as 1920, when a newspaper editorial informed Robert Goddard that a rocket could not possibly work in space.

Space starts at 100 kilometres, and below that you are not in it.

The Kármán line is a convention, chosen because it is round and roughly where aerodynamic flight stops being possible. The atmosphere does not end there; it thins out over thousands of kilometres. The Space Station orbits at about 400 km and still loses altitude to drag continuously, which is why it needs reboosting several times a year.

Voyager 1 has left the Solar System.

It left the heliosphere in 2012 — the bubble of solar wind and magnetic field the Sun inflates around itself. The Sun's gravitational domain extends vastly further: the Oort Cloud is thought to reach something like 100,000 AU, and Voyager 1 is at 174. Both statements are correct, which is why the 2013 headlines and the mission scientists appeared to be describing different events.

Webb replaced Hubble.

They observe different light and both are working. Hubble covers ultraviolet through near-infrared; Webb starts in the near-infrared and runs out to 28 µm. Hubble can see wavelengths Webb physically cannot, and Webb was built cold specifically so it could see wavelengths Hubble's warm optics drown in their own glow. The overlap between them is narrow and mostly deliberate, for cross-calibration.

Landing on Mars is much like landing on the Moon.

It is the worst of both. The Moon has no atmosphere, so you arrive slowly and brake with rockets the whole way. Earth has plenty, so a heat shield and parachutes do nearly all the work. Mars has one per cent of Earth's — enough to require a heat shield and to make the approach aerodynamically unstable, nowhere near enough to slow you to a survivable speed. Everything that has landed there needed a heat shield and a chute and a powered or airbag-cushioned final stage.

We have lost the ability to go to the Moon.

The knowledge survived; the production line did not. Saturn V tooling was scrapped, the supplier base dispersed, and the specific engineers retired — so the hardware genuinely has to be built again rather than restarted, which is most of why Artemis has taken as long as it has. The physics was never the obstacle. Artemis II flew that trajectory in April 2026.

Two that are true and sound false
  • The Apollo Guidance Computer had about four kilobytes of writable memory. It flew nine crews to the Moon on roughly two kilowords of erasable storage and a clock speed near two megahertz, with a rope-core program woven by hand. The constraint that mattered was never processing power; it was that the software had to be correct.
  • Nothing on this page travelled in a straight line. Every trajectory here is an orbit — a fall around the Sun that happens to intersect a destination. Spacecraft do not steer towards planets; they are placed on a path that arrives, and then corrected by a few metres per second at a time.
