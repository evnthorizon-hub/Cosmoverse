01 Overview

Astronomy of things that only happen once

A cosmic event is a transient: something with a beginning and an end inside a window we can actually watch. For most of its history astronomy was the study of a fixed sky, and the objects that refused to stay fixed were nuisances. They turned out to be where most of the physics is.

Everything on this page is the same sentence with different nouns: something had energy stored in it, and a threshold was crossed, and the energy came out faster than it went in. There are only three places that stored energy can have been. Gravity holds matter apart from where it wants to be. Nuclear binding holds light elements apart from the heavier ones they could become. Magnetic fields hold themselves in shapes they would rather not be in.

A supernova spends the first. A thermonuclear supernova spends the second. A solar flare spends the third. A neutron-star merger spends all three at once, which is why it produces light, gravitational waves and gold in the same afternoon.

Finding them is its own discipline. You cannot point a telescope at a supernova that has not happened yet, so transient astronomy works by subtraction: photograph the same patch of sky twice and look at the difference. Almost everything cancels. What is left is either an event or a mistake, and telling those apart is most of the job.

Every other region of this site is a noun. This one is the only verb.

Four ways an event announces itself
  • Light. The obvious channel, and the slowest to interpret — by the time an optical survey flags a supernova, the explosion is weeks old.
  • Neutrinos. They barely interact, so they escape a collapsing core immediately while photons are still fighting their way out. For a nearby supernova they arrive hours early.
  • Gravitational waves. Not light and not sound. A distortion of space itself, detectable only by measuring the distance between two mirrors to a fraction of a proton's width.
  • Cosmic rays. Charged particles accelerated by shock fronts. They arrive from no particular direction, because the galaxy's magnetic field has been bending their paths for millions of years.

The flare and the merger are separated by about twenty-two orders of magnitude, which is why they need a logarithmic axis to appear in the same figure at all: on a linear scale the flare's bar would be roughly one part in ten thousand billion billion of the merger's. Two rows need reading carefully. The core-collapse figure is total energy, and about 99 per cent of it leaves as neutrinos, 1 per cent as kinetic energy and a hundredth of a per cent as light. The gamma-ray burst figure is isotropic-equivalent: it is what the energy would have to be if the burst radiated in every direction, and it does not — it is beamed into jets a few degrees wide, so the true release is one hundred to one thousand times smaller. That row is a projection, not a measurement, and it is included because the projected number is the one usually quoted.

02 Supernovae

It begins as an implosion

The word suggests something blowing outward, and the outward part is real but it is the second half. A massive star dies because its core stops being able to hold itself up, falls inward at a quarter of the speed of light, hits a wall made of its own density, and rebounds. Everything visible is the rebound.

02a Core collapse

Type II, Ib, Ic — a star runs out

A star above roughly eight solar masses builds heavier and heavier ash in its core until that ash is iron, at which point fusion cannot pay for itself any more. With nothing generating outward pressure the core collapses in well under a second, halts abruptly when neutrons refuse to be compressed further, and rebounds into the infalling material above it. The subtypes differ only in how much of the outer envelope the star had already lost before it died.

02b Thermonuclear

Type Ia — a dead star is overfed

A completely different mechanism that happens to look similar from a distance. A white dwarf — already dead, already degenerate — pulls matter from a companion or merges with another white dwarf until it approaches about 1.4 solar masses. Carbon ignites everywhere almost at once and the entire star is unbound. Nothing is left. Because the trigger mass is always about the same, so is the peak brightness, which is what made these the standard candles that revealed cosmic acceleration in 1998.

SN 1987A — the one that was caught early
  • The neutrinos arrived first. On 23 February 1987, three detectors independently recorded a burst of about two dozen neutrinos over roughly thirteen seconds. The optical brightening followed some hours later, exactly as predicted: neutrinos leave the collapsing core immediately, light has to wait for the shock to reach the surface.
  • It was in the Large Magellanic Cloud, about 168,000 light years away — the closest supernova observed since the invention of the telescope, and still the only one whose neutrinos have been detected.
  • The predicted neutron star has never been seen. For decades there was no direct sign of the compact remnant under the debris. Infrared observations have since found a hot spot consistent with one, but the object itself remains the longest-running loose end in the subject.

03 Mergers

Two dead stars fall together and space rings like a bell

A pair of compact objects in a tight orbit slowly loses energy to gravitational waves, spirals inward, and in its last fraction of a second releases more power than every star in the observable universe combined. None of it is light. It is the geometry of space itself, oscillating.

The detection problem is absurd and worth stating plainly. A gravitational wave passing through Earth changes distances by a fraction of about 10⁻²¹. LIGO measures this by bouncing laser light along two perpendicular four-kilometre arms and watching for a difference between them — a difference of roughly a thousandth of the width of a proton. Two detectors 3,000 km apart must see the same thing, milliseconds apart, or it is discarded.

The reward for that effort is a kind of astronomy that does not need light. Gravitational waves pass through dust, through stars, through everything, undimmed. They carry information about strong-field gravity that no telescope can reach.

GW170817 — the event that started multi-messenger astronomy
  • Two neutron stars, 130 million light years away. On 17 August 2017 the wave signal ran for about a hundred seconds — far longer than a black-hole merger, because neutron stars are lighter and spiral in more slowly.
  • A gamma-ray burst followed 1.7 seconds later. Fermi caught it independently. That single coincidence confirmed that at least some short gamma-ray bursts are neutron-star mergers, a suspicion decades old.
  • Then roughly seventy observatories found the afterglow. The fading glow was a kilonova, and its spectrum showed freshly synthesised heavy elements — direct evidence that mergers are where a large share of the universe's gold, platinum and uranium is made.
  • One event, four channels. Gravitational waves, gamma rays, visible light and radio, from the same source. That is what multi-messenger means, and before 2017 it had never been done.

04 Gamma-ray bursts

Found by accident, while watching for nuclear tests

In the late 1960s the Vela satellites were looking for gamma rays from clandestine weapons tests. They kept recording flashes that came from the wrong direction: not from Earth, and not from the Sun. It took thirty years to establish that these were the most energetic explosions in the universe, going off in distant galaxies several times a day.

A gamma-ray burst is a relativistic jet pointed almost exactly at us. Some catastrophe at the centre — a collapsing stellar core, or two neutron stars finishing each other off — launches material outward at more than 99.99 per cent of the speed of light, collimated into a narrow cone. If that cone happens to intersect Earth, we see a burst. If it does not, we see nothing at all.

That beaming is why the quoted energies sound impossible, and why they should be treated carefully. The burst's brightness is measured, and then converted into a total energy on the assumption that it was radiating equally in all directions. It was not. Correcting for a jet a few degrees wide brings the figure down by a factor of a hundred to a thousand — still comfortably the largest explosions known, but not the absurdity in the headline.

The durations sort into two groups with a gap around two seconds, and the gap turned out to be real: two entirely different kinds of catastrophe, distinguishable by nothing more than a stopwatch.

Two records worth knowing
  • GRB 221009A, the brightest ever recorded. On 9 October 2022 it saturated detectors that had been designed with a generous margin, produced photons above 10 TeV — the first time such energies had been seen from a burst — and measurably disturbed Earth's ionosphere. Its light had been travelling for about 1.9 billion years.
  • GRB 080319B, briefly visible to the naked eye. Its optical flash peaked around fifth magnitude, which means that for about half a minute an unaided human eye could in principle have seen an explosion 7.5 billion light years away.

Long bursts

Collapsar

The core of a very massive, rapidly rotating star collapses straight to a black hole while the rest of the star is still in place. The jet has to drill its way out through the overlying envelope, and the seconds that takes are the seconds the burst lasts. They occur in star-forming galaxies, and several have been caught with a stripped-envelope supernova appearing underneath them days later — the same death, seen twice.

Short bursts

Compact merger

Two neutron stars, or a neutron star and a black hole, finishing a spiral that began millions of years earlier. There is no envelope for the jet to escape, so the whole thing is over in under two seconds. They turn up in old galaxies with little star formation, often well away from the centre — which fits a binary that has had a very long time to drift. GW170817 settled the argument.

05 Flares and CMEs

The same physics, close enough to break things

Twenty-two orders of magnitude below a black-hole merger sits the only event on this page that has ever inconvenienced anybody. The Sun's magnetic field gets twisted by its own rotation, stores energy in that twist, and periodically lets go — as a flash of X-rays, as a cloud of plasma, or both.

05a The scale

A, B, C, M, X

Flares are classified by peak soft X-ray flux measured at Earth, on a scale where each letter is ten times the one before it and the number within a letter is linear. An X2 is twice an X1 and twenty times an M1. The largest ever measured, on 4 November 2003, saturated the GOES detectors outright; extrapolation from the saturated data gave X28, and a later analysis that used Earth's own ionosphere as the detector put it nearer X45.

05b The consequence

It is an engineering problem

A flare is radiation and a CME is matter, and it is the matter that matters. When a cloud carrying its own magnetic field hits Earth's, the resulting geomagnetic storm induces currents in anything long and conductive — transmission lines, pipelines, undersea cables. In 1859 that meant telegraph operators receiving messages on disconnected batteries. In March 1989 it meant the Hydro-Québec grid collapsing in about ninety seconds and staying down for nine hours.

06 Misconceptions

Six things that sound right and are not

This region attracts more folklore than any other on the site, because explosions are the part of astronomy that fiction got to first.

A supernova is an outward explosion from the start.

A core-collapse supernova begins as an implosion. The iron core falls inward at something like a quarter of the speed of light, is halted when neutrons resist further compression, and rebounds. The outward blast is the bounce, helped along by neutrinos depositing energy in the material above. Getting that sequence right also explains the timing: the neutrinos escape immediately and the light takes hours to appear.

Solar flares could scorch the planet.

They cannot, and the reason is boring: the atmosphere absorbs the X-rays and the magnetosphere deflects most of the particles. Even the largest flare ever recorded produced no radiation hazard at the surface. The real exposure is to technology — power grids, satellite electronics, high-frequency radio, satellite navigation — and to astronauts outside a magnetosphere. A Carrington-class storm today would be an expensive, disruptive infrastructure emergency, not a biological one.

Gravitational waves travel through space.

They are space, oscillating. Nothing is moving through anything: the distances between things change, alternately stretching one direction and squeezing the perpendicular one. This is also why the chirp is not a sound, even though it is routinely converted to audio and played — a useful demonstration that has quietly convinced a lot of people that LIGO has a microphone.

Betelgeuse dimmed in 2019, so it is about to explode.

The Great Dimming was dust, not doom. The star ejected a large piece of its own photosphere; the material cooled and condensed into a cloud that sat between us and the surface. Betelgeuse will explode, and it will be spectacular, but the expected timescale is on the order of a hundred thousand years and at roughly 550 to 650 light years it is far too distant to endanger anything here.

Gamma-ray bursts are the most energetic explosions in the universe.

Almost. The usual figures are isotropic-equivalent — what the energy would be if the burst shone equally in all directions. It does not; it is beamed into a jet of a few degrees, so the true energy is one hundred to one thousand times lower than quoted. They remain the most energetic electromagnetic events known, but by power radiated, a black-hole merger's gravitational waves beat every one of them — and emit no light at all.

A nearby supernova would sterilise Earth.

It would have to be very near. Published estimates of the radius at which a core-collapse supernova would seriously deplete the ozone layer range from about 25 to 160 light years, depending on the model and on which effect is being counted — the honest answer is that the number is not settled. The nearest plausible candidate of any kind is the white dwarf in IK Pegasi at roughly 150 light years, and it is not expected to do anything for millions of years, by which time it will have drifted elsewhere.

Two that are true and sound false
  • Somewhere in the observable universe, a star is exploding several hundred times a second. The arithmetic is simple enough to check: a couple of trillion galaxies, at very roughly one supernova per galaxy per century, works out to a few hundred per second. Nothing about a supernova is rare except the chance of one being nearby.
  • You are wearing an event. Gold and platinum are made almost entirely by rapid neutron capture, and the strongest evidence points to neutron-star mergers as the dominant site. The metal in a wedding ring was assembled during the last seconds of two dead stars falling into each other.
