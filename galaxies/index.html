01 Overview

Not collections of stars. Structures that hold them

A galaxy is not a region of space that happens to contain a lot of stars. It is a gravitationally bound system with a shape, a rotation, a history and an age — and the stars are the least of its mass. Roughly six times more of it is material that has never been seen, only weighed.

For most of recorded astronomy these were called nebulae — faint smudges assumed to be clouds inside our own system of stars. The argument about whether they were nearby or impossibly distant ran for over a century and was settled in 1924, when Edwin Hubble found a Cepheid variable in the Andromeda “nebula” and used its known brightness to put it far outside the Milky Way.

That single measurement changed the size of the known universe by a factor of a thousand overnight. Every smudge in every catalogue became a separate system of billions of stars, and our own galaxy stopped being the universe and became one object in it.

What follows is built in that order: what a galaxy is made of, what kinds there are, how they turn, why the turning does not add up, and what has to be there to fix it.

Four numbers worth holding on to
  • Two trillion. The most-cited estimate for how many galaxies the observable universe holds — though see the last section, because that figure is contested.
  • 85 per cent. The fraction of all matter that is dark. Every galaxy on this page is mostly something we cannot detect except by its gravity.
  • Ten to the eleven. A typical large galaxy's stellar count, give or take an order of magnitude in both directions.
  • Almost never collide. When two galaxies merge, essentially no stars hit each other. The gas does; the stars just pass through.

02 Anatomy

Five parts, and only one of them is bright

A spiral galaxy is a very thin disk inside a very round halo, with a bulge of old stars at the middle and a black hole at the exact centre. Seen from above you would never guess how flat it is; seen edge-on you would never guess it has arms. This is the edge-on view.

Why the two populations are different colours
  • The bulge and halo are old. Star formation there finished billions of years ago. Every massive blue star that was ever in them has long since died, leaving the low-mass, long-lived, red ones — so the integrated light is warm ivory through amber.
  • The arms are now. The disk still has cold gas, so it is still making stars, including the rare heavy ones that burn blue-white and last only a few million years. They never get far from where they formed, which is why they trace the arms so sharply.
  • That is the whole reason a spiral looks like a gold coin wrapped in blue thread. It is not artistic licence and it is not false colour. Every galaxy render on this page is built from it.

03 Types

Four shapes, and one is just a spiral that stopped

Galaxies sort into a small number of forms, and the thing that separates them is not size or age but whether they still have cold gas. Gas means star formation, star formation means blue arms, and a galaxy that has lost its gas keeps its disk but loses its pattern.

04 Spiral structure

The arms are a traffic jam, not an object

If spiral arms were made of a fixed set of stars they would have wound themselves into a tight coil billions of years ago, because the inside of a disk goes round far more often than the outside. They have not. The arms are a wave of compression that the stars pass through, and it turns at its own speed.

The problem was noticed long before it was solved. At the Sun's distance the galaxy takes about 230 million years to turn once; a star a third of the way out takes far less. Over the ten billion years the disk has existed, the inner regions have completed dozens more circuits than the outer ones, and any pattern made of fixed material would have been wound into a spiral so tight it would be indistinguishable from a smooth disk.

The resolution, worked out by Lin and Shu in the mid-1960s, is that the arms are a standing density wave. Gas drifting into the wave crest is compressed, collapses, and lights up as new stars; the stars drift out the other side and fade. The bright arm is not a group of stars travelling together, it is a place where stars are always being made.

Three consequences that check out
  • Arms should be blue on their leading edge. Gas enters, is compressed, and takes time to ignite — so the youngest stars sit slightly ahead of the dust lane. They do.
  • There should be a radius where pattern and material agree. At the corotation radius the stars keep pace with the wave and stop being swept through it. Star formation there is measurably lower.
  • Bars should drive the whole thing. A bar is a much stronger, longer-lived density wave, and barred spirals have more sharply defined arms. Ours is barred.

05 Rotation

Two disks, same mass, and only one behaves

Put the mass where the light is and Newtonian gravity makes a firm prediction: past the bright part of the disk, orbital speed must fall away as the square root of distance, exactly as the outer planets move more slowly than the inner ones. Every spiral galaxy ever measured refuses to do this.

Measured · 21 cm hydrogenFlat. The rim is doing the same speed as the inside, and nothing about the light explains it.

Both panels are anchored to the same 220 km/s at 8 kpc — the Sun's radius, where the two models agree — and the predicted curve then falls as v = 220 √(8/r), reaching 114 km/s at the rim. Measured speeds stay flat instead, so the outermost marker completes an orbit in half the predicted time. Vera Rubin and Kent Ford established this for dozens of spirals through the 1970s; it holds for every one measured since.

06 Rotation curve

The curve that should fall, and does not

Plot orbital speed against distance from the centre and the disagreement stops being an impression and becomes a measurement. The two curves are drawn from the same galaxy: one from the gravity of everything that shines, one from the Doppler shift of hydrogen. They part company at the edge of the bright disk and never come back together.

Orbital speed is a direct measure of enclosed mass. If nearly all of a galaxy's mass sits in its bright inner region, then a star further out is orbiting a nearly fixed total, and its speed must drop off as the square root of its distance — the same law that has Neptune crawling round the Sun at a fifth of Mercury's speed.

The curves stay flat instead. For speed to hold constant while radius grows, enclosed mass has to keep growing in direct proportion to radius, out into regions where the starlight has effectively stopped. Something is there, in quantity, arranged in a roughly spherical distribution far larger than the disk, and it neither emits nor absorbs light at any wavelength we have looked in.

Vera Rubin and Kent Ford measured this galaxy after galaxy through the 1970s. The result was initially treated as a curiosity about spiral disks. It is now the main reason cosmology thinks most of the universe is made of something nobody has identified.

What else it could have been
  • Faint ordinary matter. Cold gas, dim dwarfs, dead stars. Ruled out by how much of it there would have to be, and by how much deuterium the early universe made.
  • Modified gravity. Change the force law instead of adding mass. MOND fits rotation curves impressively well and struggles badly with galaxy clusters and the cosmic microwave background.
  • Measurement error. The one possibility that has been most thoroughly eliminated. The flat curve shows up in optical spectra, in 21 cm radio, in gravitational lensing and in the motions of whole clusters.
  • Dark matter. Not an explanation so much as a name for the discrepancy, and still the only candidate that fits every measurement at once.

07 Dark matter halo

The galaxy is the small bright bit in the middle of it

Fit a mass distribution to the flat rotation curve and you get a roughly spherical halo that extends many times further than the stars, contains several times more mass than everything visible, and has no detectable edge. Drawn to one scale, the galaxy you can photograph is a scratch at the centre of it.

Four independent ways the halo gets weighed
  • Rotation curves give the mass inside each radius directly, out as far as there is hydrogen to observe.
  • Satellite orbits. The Milky Way's dwarf companions swing out to hundreds of kiloparsecs, and their speeds require far more mass than the disk contains.
  • Gravitational lensing. A foreground galaxy bends the light of things behind it by an amount set by its total mass, with no assumption about what that mass is made of.
  • Escape velocity. Fast-moving halo stars are still bound to the galaxy. That puts a floor under its mass that starlight alone cannot reach.

08 Formation

Dark matter first, then everything else

Galaxies did not condense out of a smooth universe on their own. The dark matter collapsed first, because it does not feel radiation pressure and had a head start, and the gas fell into the wells it had already made. Every structure below is gas settling into a shape that was decided before the first star existed.

  1. z ≈ 20~200 Myr

    Halos collapse

    Dark matter overdensities left over from the Big Bang pull themselves together into halos. Gas falls into them and begins to cool. Nothing has ignited yet.

  2. z ≈ 6~1 Gyr

    Clumps and mergers

    The first galaxies are small, lumpy, gas-rich and forming stars furiously. They merge constantly. Nothing yet has a disk, a bulge or a spiral pattern.

  3. z ≈ 2~3.3 Gyr

    Peak star formation

    The universe makes stars faster now than at any time before or since. Angular momentum in the infalling gas flattens it, and a thick, turbulent disk settles out.

  4. z ≈ 0.5~8.6 Gyr

    The pattern appears

    The disk thins and cools enough to carry a density wave, and spiral arms become a persistent feature rather than a passing disturbance. A bar often forms at the same time.

  5. z = 013.8 Gyr

    Now

    A quiet spiral, still turning, still making stars but at a fraction of its old rate, with a few surviving satellites and the shredded remains of the ones it has already eaten.

Fig 07 Redshifts and ages use the standard cosmology; the violet wash in each frame is the dark matter halo, which is present from the first panel and grows throughout. The Milky Way's own disk is at least 12 billion years old, so for our galaxy most of this happened early and very little has happened since.

09 Mergers

Two galaxies collide and almost nothing hits anything

Stars are unimaginably far apart relative to their size. When two galaxies pass through each other the stars simply miss — the odds of any two colliding are effectively zero. What does collide is the gas, and what does the damage is tide: gravity pulling harder on the near side of a disk than the far side, until neither disk survives.

Now

Approach

The Milky Way and Andromeda are 2.5 million light years apart and closing at about 110 km/s. Both are ordinary spirals with intact disks and their own retinues of satellites.

+4 Gyr

First pass

They miss each other, but tides throw long tails of stars out of both disks and a bridge of gas stretches between them. Neither galaxy looks like a spiral any more.

+6 Gyr

Starburst

The gas clouds do collide. Shocked hydrogen collapses across both galaxies at once and star formation spikes by a factor of tens, burning through the raw material in a few hundred million years.

+10 Gyr

One elliptical

The orbits have randomised, the gas is spent, and what is left is a single smooth ellipsoid of old stars with no disk and no ongoing star formation. Both central black holes eventually merge too.

Fig 08 Timings from n-body simulations of the Local Group; the first close pass is usually put at four to five billion years from now, with the merger complete a few billion years after that. The Sun will still exist, and is unlikely to be disturbed — but the night sky will be unrecognisable, and there will be no Milky Way left to be in.

The reason ellipticals dominate the centres of galaxy clusters is that clusters are where mergers happen most often. Repeated collisions scramble ordered rotation into random orbits, strip out or exhaust the cold gas, and leave behind exactly the red, smooth, gas-poor, non-rotating objects the Hubble sequence puts at the far end of its handle.

Our own galaxy has done this before on a smaller scale. The stellar halo is full of streams — long ribbons of stars moving together on shared orbits, which are the disassembled remains of dwarf galaxies the Milky Way has already absorbed. The Sagittarius dwarf is being pulled apart right now.

Why the stars miss
  • Scale it down. If the Sun were a grain of sand, the nearest other grain would be about seven kilometres away. A galaxy is mostly empty space by an enormous margin.
  • Gas is different. Clouds are light years across rather than light seconds, so they cannot miss. They shock, compress and collapse.
  • Tides do the shaping. No contact is needed. A difference in gravitational pull across the width of a disk is enough to unwind it completely.

10 The Milky Way

A map of the one galaxy nobody has ever seen

Every other galaxy on this page can simply be photographed. Ours cannot: we are inside the disk, two-thirds of the way out, looking through it. This map is assembled from parallaxes, maser distances, gas surveys and infrared counts — and the far side is still the least certain part of it, because the bulge is in the way.

By the standard isophote measure the disk is 87,400 light years across. The familiar round number of 100,000 comes from older, more generous definitions, and the faint outer disk of gas and old stars can be traced further still — a galaxy has no edge, only a brightness at which you stop counting.

We sit in a quiet spot: not in an arm, but on the Orion Spur, a short bridge of gas and young stars between the Sagittarius and Perseus arms. The Sun has been round the galaxy perhaps twenty times since it formed, and the Solar System has been through a spiral arm on several of those laps.

11 Local Group

Eighty galaxies, and two of them are the group

Our galaxy belongs to a small, loose, gravitationally bound collection about ten million light years across. It has around eighty known members, and almost all of the mass is in the Milky Way and Andromeda. Everything else is a dwarf, and most of those orbit one of the two big spirals.

12 Scale

The word “galaxy” covers four orders of magnitude

A dwarf spheroidal with a few thousand stars and a giant elliptical with a hundred trillion are both galaxies. The category is defined by being gravitationally bound and having its own dark halo, not by size — and the range inside it is wider than most diagrams admit.

  2. Large Magellanic Cloud Irregular satellite ~32,000 ly

On a linear scale the Milky Way's bar would be about one fiftieth of IC 1101's and Segue 1 would be invisible. In real terms IC 1101 is roughly 45 times the Milky Way's diameter and Segue 1 is about one three-hundred-and-eightieth of it. IC 1101's headline figure includes a very diffuse outer halo; its bright stellar body is considerably smaller, which is why quoted sizes for it vary so much.

Where a galaxy stops being one
  • Down the bottom end the distinction between a faint dwarf galaxy and a large globular cluster gets genuinely difficult. The test used is dark matter: a dwarf galaxy's stars move too fast for their visible mass, and a globular cluster's do not.
  • Segue 1 has perhaps a thousand stars and yet appears to be several hundred times more massive than they account for. It is one of the most dark-matter-dominated objects known.
  • At the top end the limit is set by mergers. Giant ellipticals sit at the centres of clusters, where they have absorbed everything that fell in, and their outer halos blend into the cluster's own diffuse light with no clear boundary.

13 Cosmic web

Zoom out far enough and a galaxy is one pixel

Galaxies are not scattered at random. They lie along filaments, gather at the junctions where filaments cross, and leave vast regions almost completely empty. The pattern was laid down by dark matter collapsing under its own gravity, and the galaxies are only the parts of it that happened to light up.

15 Misconceptions

Eight things that sound right and are not

Most of these are not folklore. They are things that were taught, or that were true of an older model, or that are true of the diagram rather than the object.

Spiral arms are streams of stars orbiting together.

They are a density wave. Stars and gas orbit through the arm and out the other side, and the pattern turns at its own speed. If arms were made of fixed material the difference between inner and outer orbital periods would have wound them into an indistinguishable coil billions of years ago. The bright arm is a place where stars are continually being made, not a group of stars going somewhere.

The Milky Way is about 100,000 light years across.

The standard isophote measurement gives 87,400 ± 3,600 light years. The round 100,000 comes from older and more generous definitions and has stuck through repetition. Neither is wrong exactly — a galaxy has no edge, only a surface brightness at which you stop counting, and the faint outer disk can be traced considerably further.

Dark matter is a kind of exotic gas filling the galaxy.

It is not a fluid and it does not fill the disk. The evidence points to a roughly spherical halo, far larger than the visible galaxy, made of something that does not interact electromagnetically at all — so it cannot clump, cannot cool, cannot form structures and cannot collide with itself. That non-interaction is precisely why it stayed spherical while the gas flattened into a disk.

When the Milky Way and Andromeda collide, stars will crash into each other.

Essentially none will. The ratio of a star's size to the distance between stars is roughly that of a grain of sand to a seven-kilometre gap. The gas clouds are light years across and do collide, which triggers a burst of star formation; the stars themselves pass through untouched, on scrambled orbits.

Hubble's tuning fork shows how galaxies evolve.

Hubble thought so, and it does not. Galaxies do not begin as ellipticals and unwind into spirals. If anything the sequence runs backwards: spirals lose or exhaust their gas to become lenticulars, and mergers scramble disks into ellipticals. The diagram survived because the classification is useful, not because the mechanism behind it was right.

There are two trillion galaxies in the observable universe.

That is the most-quoted figure, from a 2016 extrapolation that tried to account for galaxies too faint to detect. A 2021 measurement of the sky's background light from the New Horizons spacecraft, far from the zodiacal glow, favoured only hundreds of billions. The honest answer is that the count is uncertain by about an order of magnitude and depends heavily on what you are willing to call a galaxy.

Every galaxy has a supermassive black hole at its centre.

Most large ones do, and the hole's mass correlates tightly with the bulge's — which is a real and still not fully explained relationship. But plenty of dwarf and bulgeless galaxies show no sign of one, and at least a few large galaxies appear to be missing theirs. It is a strong pattern, not a rule.

Galaxies are held together by their own stars' gravity.

Only about a twentieth of the mass holding a large galaxy together is in its stars and gas. Take the dark halo away and a spiral disk turning at the speeds we measure would simply fly apart. The visible galaxy is a passenger in a much larger gravitational structure.

Three that are true and sound false
  • Andromeda is blueshifted. Almost every galaxy is receding, but the Local Group is bound, so its members' own motions beat the expansion. Andromeda is closing at about 110 km/s.
  • The Milky Way is eating other galaxies right now. The Sagittarius dwarf is being pulled apart on its way through, and the halo carries streams of stars from earlier meals.
  • Irregular galaxies outnumber every other kind. By count they dominate. They are simply so faint that no picture of the universe looks like it is mostly irregulars.
