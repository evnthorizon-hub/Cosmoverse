01 Overview

Space, time, matter, energy — and no outside

The universe is not a container with things in it. It is the things and the container together: every particle, every field, and the geometry those particles move through, taken as one object. That is why questions of the form "what is it expanding into" have no answer — not because the answer is unknown, but because the question assumes an outside that the definition does not provide.

Two words do most of the work on this page, and they do not mean the same thing. The observable universe is the part we could in principle have received light from: a sphere centred on any given observer, with a definite radius set by how long light has had to travel and how much the space it crossed has stretched. The universe is everything, observable or not, and nothing is known about its total size except that it is at least as large as the part we can see and very probably much larger.

Everything measurable about it is consistent with two assumptions that between them are called the cosmological principle: on large enough scales it looks the same in every direction, and it looks the same from every position. The first has been tested directly — the microwave background is uniform to about one part in a hundred thousand across the whole sky. The second cannot be tested from one location, and is adopted because the alternative is to claim that we are standing somewhere special.

What follows from that pair is surprisingly strong. If there is no centre and no edge, then the recession of distant galaxies cannot be motion away from a point, and must instead be a property of the space between them. Every other section on this page is a consequence of taking that seriously.

The universe is not expanding into anything. Distance itself is getting larger.

Five numbers that fix everything else
  • 13.787 billion years. The age, from the microwave background, to a precision of about twenty million years. Every date on this page is measured from that zero.
  • 46.5 billion light years. The present distance to the furthest matter we can see. Larger than the age in light years, and section 04 is about why that is not a contradiction.
  • Flat, to within about 0.4 per cent. Parallel lines stay parallel; the angles of a large triangle add to 180 degrees. This was an open question until 2000 and is now one of the best-measured facts in the subject.
  • 2.7255 kelvin. The temperature of the sky in microwaves, everywhere, in every direction. It is the leftover heat of the moment the universe stopped being opaque.
  • Under five per cent. The fraction of the universe made of the kind of matter that atoms, stars, planets, instruments and people are made of.

01a Observable

A sphere, and it is personal

Every observer sits at the centre of their own observable universe, because every observer has had exactly the same amount of time for light to reach them. That is not a paradox and it is not a coincidence: it is what "no special place" means in practice. A galaxy ten billion light years away has its own sphere of the same radius, overlapping ours, containing regions we will never see.

01b Entire

Size unknown, and possibly unanswerable

Because space is flat to the limit of measurement, there is no observed curvature to close it back on itself, and a flat universe with no boundary is infinite. But "flat to within 0.4 per cent" is not "flat", and a very large finite universe would look flat from inside a small enough patch of it. The honest position is that the total extent is not measured, may not be measurable, and that the number often quoted for the size of the universe is always the size of the observable part.

02 The Big Bang

Not an explosion — a change of scale

The name is a joke that stuck. Fred Hoyle used it dismissively on the radio in 1949 and it outlived the theory he preferred, which is unfortunate, because almost everything wrong in the popular picture of cosmology comes from taking the two words literally.

An explosion is matter flying apart through space from a place where it was concentrated. The Big Bang was not that. It was a state in which the whole of space was hot and dense, and the whole of space then became less so. There was no centre it went off at, no empty room it expanded into, and no location you could travel to and find the site of it. It happened where you are sitting, at the same time as everywhere else.

What the theory actually says is narrower and much better supported than the name suggests: that the universe was once hot enough and dense enough that atoms could not hold together, and that it has been getting larger and cooler ever since. Run the observed expansion backwards and the temperature rises without limit. Everything after that is thermodynamics.

The sequence follows from cooling alone. Above roughly ten trillion kelvin, quarks do not bind, so there are no protons or neutrons. Below that, they do. Above about a billion kelvin, nuclei are broken apart faster than they form, so there are no elements. Below that, for a window of a few minutes, they survive — and the universe fills with hydrogen and helium in a ratio that can be calculated from first principles and then checked against the oldest gas in the sky. Above about 3,000 kelvin, electrons cannot stay attached to nuclei, so the universe is an ionised plasma and light cannot cross it. Below that, atoms form, the fog clears, and the light released at that moment is still arriving.

What the theory does not claim
  • It does not describe the beginning. The equations are reliable from roughly a trillionth of a second onward, which is where the physics has been tested in accelerators. Before that they extrapolate, and at the notional instant of zero they fail outright — general relativity and quantum mechanics give incompatible answers and neither can be trusted.
  • It does not say where the matter came from. The theory takes a hot dense state as its starting condition and works forward. Why there was anything, and why there is more matter than antimatter, are open.
  • It does not put the universe inside anything. Expansion here is a statement about distances between points, not about a boundary moving through a surrounding void.
  • It is not a theory of a single event. "Big Bang" names the whole hot dense early era and the expansion out of it, which is why cosmologists talk about the first three minutes rather than the first instant.

03 Cosmic expansion

Nothing is moving. The distances are changing

In 1929 Edwin Hubble published a plot of forty-six galaxies: how far away each one was, against how much its light was shifted towards the red. The points fell on a straight line through the origin. Twice as far, twice as fast. That single graph is the foundation of everything on this page, and its shape — a line through the origin rather than any other curve — is what rules out the galaxies simply flying apart.

The distinction between a stretch and a flight is not pedantry — it makes different predictions, and the predictions differ in ways that have been checked. If galaxies were simply moving apart through fixed space, their light would be Doppler-shifted, the shift would be capped by the speed of light, and nothing could be observed with a redshift above one. Objects with redshifts of eight, ten and fourteen have been observed. Under the stretching picture there is no cap, because nothing is moving through anything: a galaxy's recession can exceed the speed of light without anything locally exceeding it, and light emitted from such a galaxy can still reach us as long as the space between shrinks in comoving terms faster than the galaxy retreats.

The stretch also has a boundary. Expansion is a competition between the recession of space and the gravitational binding of whatever is in it, and gravity wins easily on small scales. Atoms are not growing. Earth's orbit is not lengthening. The Milky Way is not expanding, and neither is the Local Group — Andromeda is approaching us at about 110 kilometres per second and will merge with us in roughly four and a half billion years. Expansion only shows up beyond about ten million light years, where no structure is bound to anything.

One number in this section is genuinely unsettled. Measured from the microwave background, the expansion rate comes out near 67.4 kilometres per second per megaparsec. Measured from Cepheid variables and Type Ia supernovae in the nearby universe, it comes out near 73. Both measurements have shrunk their error bars for two decades and the gap has not closed. That disagreement — the Hubble tension — is the most conspicuous open problem in observational cosmology.

What "receding faster than light" does and does not mean
  • It is a real, standard consequence. Beyond a redshift of about 1.5, a galaxy's recession velocity exceeds c. This is not a fringe claim; it is what the equations give and it is in every textbook on the subject.
  • Nothing is breaking relativity. The prohibition is on anything overtaking light through space, locally. These galaxies are stationary in their own patch of space; the distance between us is what is growing.
  • They are still visible. Superluminal recession does not hide a galaxy. Photons emitted towards us keep making progress through the grid, and for a long stretch of cosmic history the grid ahead of them shrinks in comoving terms faster than the source retreats.
  • But there is a limit. Objects beyond roughly 16 billion light years today emit light that will never reach us, because acceleration keeps carrying them away faster than their photons can close the gap. That boundary — the cosmic event horizon — is not the same as the edge of the observable universe.

04 Observable universe

13.8 billion years old, and 46.5 billion light years across the radius

Read quickly, those two numbers look like an error. If nothing outruns light and light has had 13.8 billion years, surely nothing can be further than 13.8 billion light years away. The resolution is the whole subject of this page: the distance a photon travelled and the distance to where it started are not the same quantity, because the space in between did not hold still while the photon was crossing it.

Take the oldest light there is. It set out 13.8 billion years ago, from matter that was then about 42 million light years away — close, because the universe was small. It has been travelling ever since, and while it travelled, every stretch of space it had already crossed and every stretch still ahead of it grew. It arrives having spent 13.8 billion years in transit, which is why the age and the light-travel distance agree. But the matter that emitted it has been carried along by the same expansion, and is now about 46.5 billion light years away.

Both numbers are correct and they answer different questions. Light-travel distance answers "how long was this light in flight". Comoving distance answers "where is that object now". Cosmologists quote the second, because it is the one that describes the universe rather than the journey. The ratio between them — about 3.4 — is a direct measure of how much the universe has grown since that light was released.

What sets the size of the sphere is therefore not a wall and not an edge. It is a clock. The observable universe is bounded by the fact that the universe is a finite age, and its boundary — the particle horizon — grows every year. There is no reason to expect anything different on the other side of it. The horizon is a fact about us, not about the universe.

The horizon is a statement about how long we have been here, not about how big anything is.

Three horizons, routinely confused
  • The particle horizon — 46.5 billion light years. The furthest matter whose light has had time to reach us. This is what "the observable universe" means, and it expands as the universe ages.
  • The last-scattering surface — about 45.6 billion light years. Slightly inside the particle horizon, and the furthest we can see in light. Before recombination the universe was an opaque fog, so this is an optical wall, not a distance limit. Neutrinos and gravitational waves are not stopped by it.
  • The cosmic event horizon — about 16 billion light years. Much closer, and the only one of the three that is genuinely a limit. Light leaving anything beyond it today will never reach us, because accelerated expansion will keep the gap opening faster than the photon can close it. Objects inside our particle horizon can be outside our event horizon, which is why we can see galaxies we can never signal.

05 Cosmic timeline

Thirteen point eight billion years, badly proportioned

Almost every irreversible decision the universe ever made was taken in the first twenty minutes. Everything since — all the stars, all the galaxies, all the chemistry, all of geology and biology and this sentence — happened in the leftover time, using material whose proportions were fixed before the universe was half an hour old.

05a The first twenty minutes

Where the ingredients were decided

Between about three and twenty minutes, protons and neutrons fused into the lightest nuclei and then stopped, because the universe had cooled and thinned past the point where fusion could continue. The result was roughly three quarters hydrogen and one quarter helium by mass, with traces of deuterium and lithium and nothing heavier. That mixture is calculable from first principles with essentially one free parameter, and it is measured in the oldest, least contaminated gas clouds astronomers can find. The two agree. It is one of the strongest results in the subject.

05b The dark ages

A hundred million years of nothing

After recombination the universe was transparent and completely dark. The microwave background had already redshifted out of visible light, and no star had yet formed. What happened in that gap is that slight overdensities in the dark matter — the same ones printed on the microwave background as hot and cold spots — pulled gas towards themselves until the first clouds were dense enough to ignite. Those first stars then reionised the gas around them, and by about a billion years the universe was transparent to ultraviolet again. This is the era JWST was built to look at, and its earliest galaxies have come out brighter and more assembled than most models expected.

06 Cosmic web

Galaxies did not choose where to be

Map enough galaxies and they do not come out scattered. They come out on strands: long filaments meeting at dense knots, with volumes hundreds of millions of light years across in between holding almost nothing. This is the largest structure that exists, and its shape was set by density variations printed on the universe before there were any galaxies to trace them.

The structure grew by a mechanism with no cleverness in it at all. Slightly denser regions pull slightly harder, so they gather slightly more matter, so they become denser still. Left alone for thirteen billion years, that runaway turns variations of one part in a hundred thousand into the contrast between a galaxy cluster and a void. The starting variations are not hypothetical: they are photographed directly as the hot and cold spots of the microwave background, and their statistical pattern is measured well enough that the structure it grows into can be simulated and compared with surveys.

Which is where dark matter becomes unavoidable. Ordinary matter could not have done this in the time available. Before recombination it was coupled to radiation, which resisted being compressed, so its clumping was suppressed until 380,000 years in. Dark matter does not interact with light, so it was free to start collapsing much earlier, and had already built a scaffold of gravitational wells by the time the gas was released. Every filament in the figure above is a dark matter structure with luminous matter settled into it afterwards.

Four features of the web, with sizes
  • Nodes. Galaxy clusters, where filaments cross — hundreds to thousands of galaxies in a few tens of millions of light years, bound and going nowhere. These are the densest structures in the universe and the only ones that will survive its expansion indefinitely.
  • Filaments. Strands tens to hundreds of millions of light years long. Most galaxies in the universe live in one, including ours.
  • Voids. Roughly spherical regions between the strands, typically 100 to 300 million light years across, containing a small fraction of the average density. They occupy most of the volume of the universe.
  • Walls. Sheets of galaxies bounding the voids. The Sloan Great Wall spans about 1.4 billion light years, and objects of this size are at the outer limit of what a universe of this age is expected to produce — which makes them a genuine test rather than a curiosity.

07 Composition

Ninety-five per cent of it is two things we cannot name

The budget is measured well and understood badly. Three numbers account for the total energy content of the universe to within about half a per cent, and two of the three refer to substances whose identity is unknown. They are called dark not because they are black but because nobody has anything better to call them.

07a Dark matter

It gravitates, and that is all it does

Something with mass is present in and around galaxies that does not emit, absorb or scatter light. It was inferred from the rotation of galaxies, confirmed by the way clusters bend light from behind them, and is required by the microwave background and by the existence of the cosmic web on the timescale available. It is not dim ordinary matter — the amount of ordinary matter is independently fixed by the abundance of deuterium and by the shape of the CMB's peaks, and there is not enough. No particle has been detected despite forty years of increasingly sensitive experiments.

07b Dark energy

It pushes, and it does not dilute

The expansion is speeding up, which requires something with negative pressure spread evenly through space. The simplest candidate is a cosmological constant — an energy density that stays the same as space grows, so its influence increases relative to everything else as matter thins out. Measurements of its equation of state give a value indistinguishable from that constant, within a few per cent. The uncomfortable part is that the natural theoretical estimate of its size, from quantum field theory, is wrong by dozens of orders of magnitude, which is generally regarded as the worst prediction in physics.

Where the 4.9 per cent actually is
  • Almost none of it is in stars. Roughly nine tenths of the ordinary matter in the universe is diffuse ionised gas, most of it strung along filaments between the galaxies rather than inside them. Stars account for a few per cent of the ordinary matter, which puts them at a few hundredths of one per cent of the universe.
  • Planets and dust round to zero. On this budget every rocky body, every grain of interstellar dust and every atom that has ever been part of anything solid is a rounding error on a rounding error.
  • The total was fixed before the first star. How much ordinary matter exists is set by the first twenty minutes, and is measured two independent ways — from the deuterium abundance in ancient gas, and from the relative heights of the peaks in the microwave background. They agree, which is the reason nobody suspects the answer is simply "more ordinary matter we have not found".
  • Some of it went missing and was found. For years the census of ordinary matter in the nearby universe came up about a third short of what the CMB required. Most of that gap has since been closed by detecting the warm–hot intergalactic medium — gas at a million kelvin, too diffuse to see easily in any single line of sight.

08 How we know

Four measurements, and they agree with each other

None of these four probes the same physics, uses the same instruments or shares the same systematic errors. They come from different decades, different wavelengths and different objects. They land on the same age, the same geometry and the same composition, and that convergence is the actual reason the account on this page is believed.

Galaxy redshift

Hubble–Lemaître

Every element absorbs and emits at fixed wavelengths, so a galaxy's spectrum arrives carrying a barcode whose spacing is known in advance. In distant galaxies that whole barcode is displaced towards longer wavelengths, by the same proportion at every line — which is the signature of a stretch rather than a change in temperature or composition. Slipher measured the velocities from 1912, Lemaître derived the relation in 1927 and Hubble published the plot in 1929. It is the observation the expansion was deduced from, and it is still how distances beyond the local universe are assigned.

Type Ia supernovae

Standard candles

A white dwarf pushed to about 1.4 solar masses detonates, and because the trigger mass is always about the same, so is the peak brightness. That makes each one a bulb of known wattage at an unknown distance, which is exactly what is needed to measure distances independently of redshift. In 1998 two competing teams found that the distant ones came out systematically fainter than a decelerating universe allows. The expansion was speeding up. 09 Ultimate fate

It depends on one number, and we have measured it

For most of the twentieth century the fate of the universe was a question about how much matter it contained: enough to halt the expansion and reverse it, or not enough. Since 1998 the question has been a different one entirely, about whether dark energy stays constant, weakens, or strengthens. The measured answer, so far, is that it stays constant — and that answer implies expansion forever, at an accelerating rate.

If dark energy is a true constant, the long-term picture is not violent. The expansion never stops and never reverses; it simply becomes exponential, and everything not gravitationally bound is carried out of reach. In roughly a hundred billion years every galaxy outside the Local Group will have crossed our cosmic event horizon, and an observer here would see a single merged galaxy in an otherwise empty sky, with no way left to discover that the universe expands at all. The evidence this page is built on is temporary.

After that, thermodynamics runs its course over spans that make the current age irrelevant. Star formation ends when the gas is exhausted, in something like a hundred trillion years. The remaining stellar remnants cool. If protons decay — which has never been observed and may not happen — matter itself eventually disperses. Black holes evaporate by Hawking radiation, the largest of them on timescales around 10⁹⁶ years. What is left is a cold, dilute, nearly featureless expanse at a temperature approaching but never reaching zero. That end state is usually called the heat death, and it is the boring answer, which is a reasonable heuristic for it being the right one.

One caveat should be stated plainly. Since 2024 the DESI survey's baryon acoustic oscillation measurements have shown a persistent preference — not yet decisive, and not yet independently confirmed — for a dark energy whose strength changes with time rather than staying constant. If that holds up, the extrapolation above changes, and the fate becomes an open question again rather than a settled one. It is the most interesting live result in the field, and it is exactly the kind of thing that ought to be flagged rather than smoothed over.

Timescales, in order
  • ~4.5 billion years. The Milky Way and Andromeda merge. Both are bound to each other, so expansion is irrelevant to this.
  • ~100 billion years. Every galaxy outside the Local Group has receded beyond the cosmic event horizon. The observable universe becomes almost empty.
  • ~10¹⁴ years. The last stars burn out. No gas is left cold and dense enough to form new ones.
  • ~10¹⁵ years and beyond. Planetary systems have been dissolved by stellar encounters; galaxies have evaporated their own stars.
  • ~10⁹⁶ years. The largest black holes finish evaporating. This is the longest process in standard physics with a calculable end date.
  • Indefinitely. A cold, dilute, expanding space, cooling towards a temperature it never reaches.

Seven things that sound right and are not

Every one of these follows from taking the words "big bang" literally, which is a reasonable thing to do and wrong in seven separate ways.

The Big Bang was an explosion in empty space.

There was no empty space for it to happen in. The Big Bang describes the expansion of space itself, everywhere at once — a hot dense state that became a cooler sparser one. An explosion has a location, an outward direction and a surrounding void, and this had none of the three. Fig 02 is the correct picture: a grid whose spacing grows, with nothing moving through it.

There is a point in space where it happened.

It happened everywhere, including exactly where you are. If the expansion has no centre — and every measurement says it does not — then running it backwards does not converge on a place, it converges on a time when everywhere was dense. The microwave background is the clearest demonstration: it arrives from every direction at the same temperature, which is only possible if the hot early state filled the whole sky rather than sitting in one part of it.

A 13.8-billion-year-old universe cannot be 46.5 billion light years in radius.

It can, because the space the light crossed kept growing while it crossed. The photon spent 13.8 billion years in flight, which is why the light-travel distance matches the age exactly. But its source was carried along by the expansion and is now 46.5 billion light years away. Two different distances answering two different questions; section 04 separates them, and Fig 02 shows the mechanism as a stretched wave.

Galaxies are flying away from us through space.

Their actual motion through space — peculiar velocity — is a few hundred kilometres per second in random directions, and it is small. What produces the observed redshift is the growth of the distance between us, not travel. The clearest single counterexample is on this page: Andromeda is approaching at about 110 kilometres per second, because it is bound to us and expansion does not act inside bound systems.

If everything is expanding, atoms and orbits are expanding too.

They are not, and the reason is simple: gravity and the other forces win easily on small scales. Expansion is only detectable where nothing is bound — beyond about ten million light years. Earth's orbit is not lengthening, the Solar System is not growing, the Milky Way is not expanding, and neither is the Local Group. A ruler stays the same length, which is what makes the expansion measurable at all.

The microwave background is light from the Big Bang.

It is light from 380,000 years afterwards — the moment the universe cooled enough for atoms to form and the fog of free electrons to clear. Everything before that is permanently hidden in light, not because it is far away but because it is behind an opaque plasma. It is the oldest light there is, and it is nothing like the oldest thing there was.

Dark matter and dark energy are two names for the same mystery.

They do opposite things and behave in opposite ways. Dark matter has mass, gravitates, clumps into haloes and filaments, and is what pulled the cosmic web together. Dark energy has negative pressure, pushes, is smooth to the limits of measurement, and does not dilute as space grows. They are also measured by different features of the data, and the two figures are independent. The only thing they share is the adjective.

Three that are true and sound false
  • An analogue television tuned to a dead channel was showing you the early universe. A small fraction of the static in old UHF snow was genuine cosmic microwave background — the same photons Penzias and Wilson could not get rid of, arriving in a domestic aerial. Most of the noise was terrestrial and the CMB share was a few per cent, but it was there.
  • Almost every hydrogen atom in your body is 13.8 billion years old. Hydrogen was made in the first twenty minutes and has never been made in bulk since. The oxygen it is bonded to in every water molecule was made in a star, and the iron in your blood in a supernova or a neutron-star merger — but the hydrogen itself predates all of them by billions of years.
  • We can see galaxies we could never send a message to. Anything beyond about 16 billion light years today lies outside our cosmic event horizon: light we emit now will never arrive there, and light it emits now will never arrive here. We are watching photons that left before the gap became uncrossable. The observable universe and the reachable universe are different volumes, and the second is shrinking.

Nine regions, and they were one object all along

The Solar System is one star's worth of leftover disc. The stars are the objects that made the elements it is built from. The black holes are what the largest of them became, the galaxies are what they collect into, the nebulae are the material passing between those states, the cosmic events are the transitions themselves, and the missions are how any of it came to be known. All of it sits inside one filament of the web in Fig 06, in a universe 13.8 billion years old that is still getting larger as you read this.
