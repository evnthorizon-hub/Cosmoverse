01 Overview

A balance held for billions of years

A star is a ball of plasma massive enough that its own gravity crushes its core until hydrogen nuclei begin to fuse. The energy released pushes back out. When the two forces balance exactly, the star stops collapsing and simply shines — a state it can hold, if it is the right size, for longer than the universe has so far existed.

Below about 0.08 solar masses gravity never wins hard enough to start fusion, and you get a brown dwarf: warm, dim, and not a star. Above roughly 300 solar masses the outward pressure wins outright and the object tears itself apart. Everything we call a star lives inside that range.

What makes stellar astronomy tractable is that almost nothing else matters. Give the initial mass and the chemical composition, and the star's colour, size, brightness, lifetime and manner of death all follow. That is an unusually strong statement for astrophysics, and the next section is where it becomes visible.

Look at enough stars and they do not scatter. They fall into one narrow line — and that line is a sequence in mass.

What a star is not
  • Not burning. Nothing is on fire. Fusion converts mass into energy; combustion rearranges chemical bonds. The two differ by a factor of about ten million.
  • Not solid, liquid or gas. Stellar material is plasma — ionised, so it conducts and responds to magnetic fields.
  • Not fusing throughout. Fusion happens only in the innermost few per cent of the volume, where it is hot and dense enough.
  • Not steady forever. Every star is slowly changing composition as it converts hydrogen to helium. That drift is what eventually ends it.

02 Classification

Seven letters, in a famously wrong order

Stars are sorted by the absorption lines in their spectra — the dark gaps where atoms in the star's own atmosphere have removed their own wavelengths from the light passing through. The original scheme ran A to Q by hydrogen line strength. When Annie Jump Cannon reordered it by temperature in the 1900s, the surviving letters came out as O B A F G K M, and astronomy has been living with it since.

Two things the names get wrong
  • "Red" dwarfs are orange. A 3,000 K blackbody is orange. The colours on this page are the colours these stars actually are, so class M is rendered orange even though its common name says otherwise.
  • The Sun is not a yellow star. Its output peaks in green and it integrates to white. Every "yellow dwarf" you have ever seen drawn is an artefact of Earth's atmosphere.

03 The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram

Plot colour against brightness and the sky sorts itself out

In 1911 Ejnar Hertzsprung, and independently in 1913 Henry Norris Russell, plotted stars by temperature against luminosity expecting a cloud. They got a line. Around nine stars in ten fall on one narrow diagonal, and the position of a star along it is set almost entirely by its mass. Everything off the diagonal is a star that has stopped fusing hydrogen in its core.

How to read it
  • Left is hot. Temperature decreases to the right. Hertzsprung and Russell plotted spectral class along the bottom, and O came first, so the axis has run backwards ever since.
  • Up is bright. Eleven decades of luminosity, from a ten-thousandth of the Sun to a million times it.
  • The diagonal is a mass sequence. Position along the main sequence is set by mass: hot and bright at the top, cool and faint at the bottom.
  • Off the diagonal means finished. A star leaves the main sequence when its core runs out of hydrogen. Where it goes next depends on how heavy it started.
The two vertical gaps
  • Above the sequence: giants and supergiants have the same surface temperature as ordinary stars but are hundreds of times brighter. Since brightness depends on area, they must be enormous — which is how we knew stars could be that large before we could resolve one.
  • Below the sequence: Sirius B is hotter than Sirius A but 450 times fainter. The only way to be hot and dim is to be tiny. That reasoning identified white dwarfs as Earth-sized objects with the mass of a star.
  • The hatched strip marks where a star's envelope cannot find equilibrium and pulses instead. Cepheids live at its top, RR Lyrae variables lower down.

04 Formation

It starts with a cloud that is too cold to resist

Star formation is a race between gravity and pressure inside a cloud of molecular hydrogen. Where the cloud is dense and cold enough, pressure loses. What follows takes about ten million years and turns a diffuse region tens of light years across into an object the size of the Sun.

  1. t = 0Free-fall begins

    Molecular cloud

    Hydrogen at 10–20 K, dense enough for molecules to survive. Dust makes it opaque, which is why these regions look like holes in the sky. A disturbance — a passing shock, a nearby supernova — pushes part of it over the threshold.

  2. ~100,000 yrCore forms

    Collapse

    Fragments contract and fragment again. Gravitational energy converts to heat, the centre becomes opaque to its own infrared, and the trapped heat builds a genuine pressure-supported core for the first time.

  3. ~10⁵ yrAccretion

    Protostar

    Conservation of angular momentum flattens the infalling material into a disc. Magnetic fields channel some of it back out along the rotation axis as two opposed jets — the most recognisable signature of a star being built.

  4. ~10⁷ yrDisc clears

    T Tauri phase

    The star is nearly assembled but not yet fusing. It is variable, magnetically violent, and still contracting. The disc thins and develops gaps where planets are sweeping their orbits clear.

  5. ~10⁷ yrEquilibrium

    Ignition

    The core reaches about 10 million K and hydrogen fusion begins. Outward pressure finally matches gravity, contraction stops, and the star arrives on the main sequence — where it will spend ninety per cent of its life.

Fig 04 Timings are for a roughly solar-mass star. A 30 M☉ star runs the same sequence in under 100,000 years; a 0.1 M☉ star takes several hundred million.

The mass–lifetime inversion

A massive star has far more fuel and burns through it far faster. Luminosity rises roughly as the fourth power of mass while fuel rises only linearly, so lifetime collapses. This is the only ledger on CosmoVerse where more of the quantity produces a shorter bar — and that inversion is the entire point.

Consequence
  • Every O-type star you can see was formed within the last few million years. They cannot be older; they do not last.
  • No red dwarf has ever died. The universe is 13.8 billion years old and their lifetimes run into the trillions, so every one ever formed is still shining.

07 Binary & variable stars

Most stars come in pairs

Single stars like the Sun are the minority among massive stars. Pairs are extraordinarily useful: if two stars orbit each other, Kepler's third law gives their masses directly — and stellar mass is otherwise almost impossible to measure.

An eclipsing pair

Both stars orbit a common barycentre, not each other. The more massive star circles closer in: Algol A is about 4.5 times the mass of Algol B and so keeps a 4.5 times tighter orbit. Seen nearly edge-on, each one passes in front of the other once per revolution.

Reading brightness over time

A light curve is the whole of observational variable-star astronomy in one plot. Its shape identifies the mechanism: two unequal dips per cycle means an eclipse, a sawtooth means the star itself is pulsing.

Kinds of binary
  • Visual — far enough apart to resolve as two points. Alpha Centauri A and B.
  • Spectroscopic — unresolvable, but the spectral lines shift back and forth as the stars approach and recede.
  • Eclipsing — the orbit happens to lie edge-on to us, so the pair periodically dims. The only kind that gives radii directly.
  • Contact — so close that the two share an envelope and material flows from one to the other.
Why Cepheids matter more than they should
  • In 1908 Henrietta Swan Leavitt found that a Cepheid's pulsation period predicts its true luminosity.
  • Time the pulse and you know how bright it really is. Compare that with how bright it looks, and you have its distance.
  • Edwin Hubble used exactly this on Cepheids in Andromeda in 1923 and proved it was a separate galaxy, not a nebula inside ours.
  • The method is still the first rung of the cosmic distance ladder a century later.
