Near is not the same as bright

Apparent magnitude measures how bright a star looks; absolute magnitude measures how bright it actually is, standardised to 10 parsecs. Compare the two columns below and the sky rearranges itself: the stars that dominate our night are mostly distant giants, while our actual neighbours are almost all invisible.

Reading the columns
  • V mag — apparent visual magnitude. Lower is brighter, and the scale is logarithmic: five magnitudes is a factor of exactly 100 in brightness. Negative values are very bright.
  • Abs. mag — what the star's magnitude would be at a standard 10 parsecs (32.6 ly). This strips distance out, so it is a true comparison of output.
  • Type — spectral class, subclass, then luminosity class in Roman numerals: V main sequence, IV subgiant, III giant, II bright giant, I supergiant. D prefixes mark degenerate stars, meaning white dwarfs.
  • Mass and radius — relative to the Sun. Radius is the least certain figure for supergiants, which have no sharp surface.
What the table shows
  • Only three stars are on both lists. Sirius A, α Centauri A and Procyon A are both nearby and genuinely bright. Every other prominent star in the sky is prominent because it is enormous, not because it is close.
  • Eleven of the sixteen nearest are class M and not one of them is visible to the naked eye. The nearest star to the Sun sits at magnitude 11.
  • Deneb is the extreme case. It is 2,615 light years away — over 300 times further than Sirius — and still one of the twenty brightest stars in the sky.
  • Absolute magnitude sorts them properly. Read that column alone and the giants separate from the dwarfs by more than twenty magnitudes: a factor of a hundred million in output.
Sources and precision
  • Distances for nearby stars come from parallax, principally Gaia and Hipparcos. These are accurate to better than a per cent within a few dozen light years.
  • Distances beyond a few hundred light years are much softer. Values for Betelgeuse (~548 ly, NASA), Rigel, Antares, Polaris and Deneb carry uncertainties of ten per cent or more, and the derived absolute magnitudes inherit that.
  • Absolute magnitudes here are computed from the listed apparent magnitude and distance rather than copied, so the columns are internally consistent. They are uncorrected for interstellar extinction, which matters most for the distant O and B stars.
  • Variable stars — Betelgeuse, Antares, Polaris, Achernar and several M dwarfs — are listed at a representative magnitude, not a fixed one.
  • Multiple systems are listed by component where the components are resolved and physically distinct, so Sirius, Procyon and α Centauri each appear more than once.
