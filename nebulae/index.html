01 Overview

A cloud thinner than any vacuum we can build

Nebula is simply Latin for cloud, and for most of astronomy's history it meant any fuzzy patch that refused to resolve into stars. The name survived the discovery that half of those patches were entire galaxies. What is left under it is genuine: interstellar gas and dust, gathered densely enough to be seen against the sky.

A nebula is about 99 per cent gas by mass — roughly seventy parts hydrogen to twenty-eight parts helium, with everything heavier making up the remainder — and about 1 per cent dust: grains of silicate and carbon a fraction of a micron across. That 1 per cent does most of the visible work, because grains block and scatter light far more effectively per gram than gas does.

The densities involved are the hardest thing to believe. A bright emission nebula holds somewhere between a hundred and ten thousand particles per cubic centimetre. The air in the room you are in holds about twenty-five billion billion. A good ultra-high-vacuum chamber gets down to a few million per cubic centimetre, which means it is emptier than the Orion Nebula by a comfortable margin.

They are visible anyway, because they are enormous. The Orion Nebula is around twenty-four light years across and holds roughly two thousand solar masses of gas. Stack a hundred trillion kilometres of almost nothing along a line of sight and it stops being almost nothing. Flying through one, you would notice nothing at all.

Not a substance you could touch. A column depth you happen to be looking down.

Three ways a cloud becomes visible
  • It emits. A hot star strips electrons from the hydrogen around it. When they recombine, the gas gives out light at fixed wavelengths of its own.
  • It scatters. With no ionising star nearby, dust grains simply redirect starlight — preferentially at the blue end, so the cloud comes out bluer than the star lighting it.
  • It blocks. With nothing behind it to light it and something bright behind it to hide, the same dust is a silhouette.

02 Types

Five names, and three are one cloud

Emission, reflection and dark nebulae are not three kinds of object. They are the same interstellar cloud under three lighting conditions, and which one you see depends entirely on what star happens to be near it and where you happen to be standing. The other two are different: they are what a star leaves behind when it dies.

  1. 01

    Emission

    H II region

    A cloud with a hot young star inside it. Ultraviolet light shorter than 91.2 nm carries enough energy to strip the electron off a hydrogen atom, and when the two find each other again the atom emits at its own fixed wavelengths. So the gas is genuinely a light source, not a reflector. The characteristic rose is Hα; the teal in the hottest parts is doubly ionised oxygen.

  2. 02

    Reflection

    No ionisation

    The same dust, near a star that is bright but not hot enough to ionise anything. The grains simply redirect its light, and they are better at redirecting short wavelengths than long ones — so the cloud comes out distinctly bluer than the star illuminating it, for the same reason the daytime sky is blue. Vesto Slipher settled this in 1912 by taking a spectrum of the haze around the Pleiades and finding the stars' own absorption lines in it.

  3. 03

    Dark

    Absorption only

    The same dust again, with nothing nearby to light it and something bright behind it to hide. William Herschel took these for genuine holes in the Milky Way; they are the opposite — the densest material in the neighbourhood. They matter far more than their looks suggest, because a cloud cold and opaque enough to be black is a cloud cold enough to collapse. Every star forms inside one.

  4. 04

    Planetary

    Stellar death · low mass

    Nothing to do with planets. William Herschel named them in 1785 for their round, planet-like appearance in a small telescope and the name stuck. What they actually are is the outer envelope of a dying star of roughly 0.8 to 8 solar masses, shrugged off over a few thousand years and then lit from inside by the exposed core — a future white dwarf at anywhere from 25,000 to 200,000 K. Here the geometry changes: this is a shell, with a centre.

  5. 05

    Supernova remnant

    Stellar death · high mass

    The other shell, and the violent one. A star above roughly eight solar masses does not shrug its envelope off; it throws it outward at thousands of kilometres per second, and the wreckage ploughs into whatever was already there. That shock is what makes remnants filamentary rather than smooth, and it is where most of the elements heavier than iron in your body were distributed from.

03 Star birth

Stars begin in the coldest places there are

Star formation happens in giant molecular clouds: tens to hundreds of light years across, thousands to millions of solar masses, and sitting at ten to twenty degrees above absolute zero. The cold is not incidental. It is the precondition.

Gravity is always trying to collapse a cloud, and gas pressure is always resisting. Pressure comes from heat, so the colder a cloud is, the less it can push back — and below about twenty kelvin, hydrogen stops being atomic and pairs up into H₂ molecules that radiate warmth away efficiently instead of trapping it. A cloud that can shed its heat can keep collapsing, which is why nothing forms in warm gas.

Molecular hydrogen at that temperature is also almost impossible to detect, having no convenient emission lines. Nearly everything known about these clouds was measured through carbon monoxide instead: about one CO molecule per ten thousand H₂, bright at millimetre wavelengths, and standing in for the hydrogen it travels with.

Once a dense core exceeds its own Jeans mass it collapses, and it collapses unevenly — which is why stars are born in groups rather than one at a time. Only a few per cent of a cloud's mass ever ends up inside stars. The rest is blown away by the ones that made it.

The cloud is the parent of everything else
  • Dark nebulae are the nurseries. Type 03 in the section above and this section are the same object described twice — once by how it looks, once by what it does.
  • The nursery destroys itself. The massive stars a cloud produces ionise and blow away the gas around them. An H II region is a cloud being dismantled by its own children.
  • That is why bright nebulae are young. A visible H II region lasts a few million years at most. On astronomical timescales, every one you can see switched on recently.

04 Four we know best

Two nurseries and two kinds of corpse

Between them these four cover everything above: the nearest region where massive stars are being made, the most photographed dust in the sky, the only nebula that can be matched to a specific night in recorded history, and a shell of gas that is the future of the Sun.

01 / 04 M42 · NGC 1976

Orion

Emission

The nearest place massive stars are forming, and the only nebula easily visible to the unaided eye — the middle “star” in Orion's sword. Not a sphere but a blister on the near face of a much larger molecular cloud: the Trapezium stars have eaten a cavity outward towards us, which is why we can see in at all.

Eagle

An H II region whose most famous feature is dark, not bright. Hubble's 1995 frame is probably the most reproduced astronomical image ever taken.

Crab

Remnant

The wreckage of a star seen to explode in 1054, recorded by Chinese and Japanese observers, and the first nebula ever tied to a specific historical event. Unusually it is filled rather than hollow: the pulsar at its centre, spinning about thirty times a second, keeps pumping energy into the interior, which glows blue-white by synchrotron radiation.

05 Misconceptions

Six things that sound right and are not

Most of these are not folklore. They are things that were taught, or that were true of an older model, or that are true of the picture rather than the object.

Nebulae are thick, billowing clouds you could fly into.

They are the emptiest things you will ever see. A bright nebula holds a hundred to ten thousand particles per cubic centimetre against the twenty-five billion billion in ordinary air, and a good laboratory vacuum is thinner still. Flying through one, you would register nothing: no haze, no resistance, no colour. They look substantial only because you are looking through light years of them at once.

The colours in the famous images are what your eye would see.

Partly. Hα genuinely is rose and [O III] genuinely is teal-green, as Fig 01 shows. But most celebrated nebula images are narrowband composites in which each emission line is assigned a channel for legibility — the standard Hubble palette puts [S II] in red, Hα in green and [O III] in blue, which is why the Pillars come out gold and teal rather than pink. The structure is real; the mapping is a decision.

Planetary nebulae have something to do with planets.

Nothing whatsoever. William Herschel coined the term in 1785 because in the telescopes of the day they were small round discs that looked like planets. They are dying stars of roughly one to eight solar masses shedding their outer layers. The name is a two-hundred-year-old observational accident that astronomy has never got around to fixing.

Nebulae glow because they are hot.

Three different things are going on and only one involves heat. An emission nebula's gas is around ten thousand kelvin, but it does not shine as a hot body does — it shines because ionised atoms are recombining and emitting at specific wavelengths. A reflection nebula produces no light of its own at all. A dark nebula is ten to twenty degrees above absolute zero, which is about as cold as anything gets.

Nebulae are permanent features of the sky.

They are the most short-lived structures on this site. A planetary nebula disperses in ten to twenty thousand years. A supernova remnant merges into the surrounding medium within a hundred thousand or so. A bright H II region survives a few million years before the stars inside it blow it apart. Against a 13.8-billion-year universe, every nebula you can see appeared essentially just now.

The Pillars of Creation were destroyed a thousand years ago.

This is a hypothesis, not a finding, and it is repeated as though it were settled. A 2007 Spitzer interpretation of hot dust near the pillars was read as evidence of a shock front that had already passed through them, whose arrival we would witness in about a thousand years. It has not been confirmed, other explanations for that dust exist, and later infrared imaging shows the pillars intact. The honest position is that we do not know.

Two that are true and sound false
  • Half the entries in the first galaxy catalogues were nebulae, and half the nebulae were galaxies. Andromeda was the “Andromeda Nebula” until 1924, when Edwin Hubble found a Cepheid variable in it and put it far outside the Milky Way. The word nebula simply meant “unresolved smudge”, and it took a century to sort the clouds from the galaxies.
  • You are made of nebula. Every atom in you heavier than helium was manufactured inside a star and then distributed by one of the two death geometries in section 02. The Solar System condensed out of gas that had already been through that cycle several times.
