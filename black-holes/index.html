The render above is not an artist's impression. Its geometry comes from the same physics that produced the black hole in Interstellar — Kip Thorne wrote the equations, Double Negative built the renderer, and the result was accurate enough to yield two published papers.

Everything strange about the image is a real prediction. The disk appears to pass over the top of the black hole because you are seeing its far side, with light bent through almost 180° around the hole and delivered back to your eye. The bright rim hugging the shadow is light that orbited the hole one or more times before escaping. The black disc in the middle is 2.6 times wider than the event horizon it contains, because the hole magnifies its own silhouette.

What you cannot see is the black hole itself. Every photon in the frame comes from gas outside it. A black hole has no surface, no substance and nothing to look at — only a region that light enters and does not leave.

Three things this page will not do
  • Colour the disk. Accretion disks are thermal emission at millions of kelvin. They read white with amber edges. Every purple or cyan black hole you have seen was a choice made for a poster.
  • Put a texture on the shadow. It is #000000 everywhere on this page. It occludes the starfield completely, because there is nothing there to emit.
  • Call it a hole in space. It is a region of spacetime, not a puncture in it. Nothing is torn, nothing leads anywhere, and it has a definite mass, spin and charge — three numbers, and that is the entire description.
One thing the film left out on purpose
  • A real disk is lopsided. The side rotating toward you is Doppler-boosted and can be many times brighter than the side rotating away. Thorne's simulations showed this correctly — and the filmmakers switched it off, because a black hole with one blazing edge read as a rendering error to test audiences. The disk here is symmetric for the same reason, and this is the one place the page departs from the physics.

01 Anatomy

Six boundaries and one place the maths stops

A black hole has no parts in the way a planet has parts. What it has is a set of nested radii, each defined by what happens to light there. Every one of them is measured in Schwarzschild radii — Rs = 2GM/c² — and for a non-rotating hole that single number fixes all of them.

The two radii people confuse
  • The event horizon sits at 1 Rs. It is not a surface or a membrane — nothing is there. It is simply the last place from which an outward-going light ray can still make progress outward.
  • The shadow is what you photograph, and it is 2.6 Rs across — 2.6 times larger. The hole bends light so strongly that rays which would have missed it are pulled in, so its silhouette is magnified. The EHT images show the shadow, not the horizon.
Only three numbers
  • A black hole is completely described by mass, spin and electric charge. Nothing else survives the collapse — not composition, not shape, not what fell in.
  • This is the no-hair theorem, and it is unique in astrophysics. Two holes with the same three numbers are not similar objects; they are identical in every measurable respect.
  • Real holes have essentially zero charge, so in practice it is two numbers. Mass sets the size; spin sets almost everything interesting about the disk and the jets.

02 Gravitational lensing

Light does not bend. The straight lines do

Photons always travel the straightest available path. Near a black hole the straightest available paths are curved, because mass curves the geometry those paths are drawn in. How far a ray is deflected depends on one quantity: its impact parameter b, the distance it would have missed by had nothing been there.

The observable consequence

Because rays passing on either side are bent toward you, a single object behind a black hole is seen more than once. Perfectly aligned, its images merge into a complete circle — an Einstein ring. This is not exotic: it is routinely observed around galaxies and galaxy clusters, and it is how a great deal of dark matter has been mapped.

Where this was settled
  • Einstein predicted a deflection of 1.75 arcseconds for starlight grazing the Sun — twice the Newtonian value.
  • Eddington's expedition measured it during the total eclipse of 29 May 1919, and general relativity stopped being a hypothesis.
  • A black hole is the same effect taken to its limit. Nothing new is happening — the geometry is just steep enough that light can be turned all the way around.

03 The event horizon

The sky closes like an aperture

"Escape velocity exceeds the speed of light" is the usual line, and it is misleading — it suggests you could get out with a fast enough rocket. The accurate picture is geometric. Point a torch outward from close to a black hole and only some directions still lead away. As you descend, that window of escaping directions narrows, and at the horizon it shuts.

Crossing it
  • You would not feel it. There is no wall, no jolt, no shudder. For a supermassive hole the horizon is a completely unremarkable place, and you would not be able to tell locally that you had passed it.
  • You could not tell anyone. Every signal you sent afterwards would fall inward with you. The horizon is defined by exactly that.
  • You would still see out. The sky outside remains visible, squeezed into a shrinking bright patch above you as you fall.
Watching from outside
  • The two observers do not agree, and both are right. From a distance you never see anything cross. The infalling object appears to slow, redden and dim, freezing ever closer to the horizon and fading below detection.
  • This is why Soviet physicists originally called them frozen stars. The name describes the view from here; "black hole" describes the geometry.
  • The falling observer measures a short, finite time and crosses without incident. Nothing is paradoxical — the two are simply using different clocks, and relativity does not privilege either.

04 Accretion disk

Friction between shells moving at a third of light speed

A black hole itself emits nothing. Everything we detect comes from the gas queueing to get in. Because inner orbits move faster than outer ones, adjacent shells of that gas grind past each other at enormous relative speeds, and the friction heats the disk until it radiates X-rays. Quasars — the brightest sustained objects in the universe — are this process around a supermassive hole.

Why the inner edge stops at 3 Rₛ
  • In Newtonian gravity there is a stable circular orbit at every radius. In general relativity there is not: below three Schwarzschild radii no stable circular orbit exists at all.
  • Gas drifting inward past that point cannot settle into a new orbit. It spirals in over a few orbits and is gone, which is why disks have a sharp inner edge and a hole in the middle.
  • Spin moves this boundary. For a maximally rotating hole the innermost stable orbit drops to about 0.5 Rₛ, the disk reaches far deeper into the gravity well, and the efficiency climbs.
The most efficient engine there is
  • Accretion converts 6% to 32% of infalling rest mass into radiation, depending on spin.
  • Hydrogen fusion — the process powering every star — manages 0.7%.
  • Chemical burning manages about 0.00000003%.
  • That is why a quasar the size of the Solar System can outshine the hundred billion stars of its host galaxy combined.

05 Relativistic jets

The one thing that gets back out

Not everything that falls toward a black hole falls in. Magnetic field lines threading the inner disk are wound up by the hole's rotation into a tight helix, and that helix flings a fraction of the infalling plasma back out along the spin axis at more than 99% of light speed. These jets are the largest coherent structures any single object makes.

Why only one jet is usually visible
  • Jets are launched in opposite pairs, and they are intrinsically near-identical.
  • The one pointed toward us is relativistically beamed — its light is concentrated forward and blueshifted, brightening it enormously. The one pointed away is beamed away and dimmed just as severely.
  • M87's jet is a textbook case: the approaching jet is a famous 5,000-light-year spike, and the counter-jet was only detected decades later.
  • The same effect makes some jets appear to move faster than light across the sky. They do not — it is a projection illusion from material chasing its own emitted light almost directly at us.
Feedback on a galactic scale
  • Jets from a supermassive hole inject enough energy into the surrounding gas to stop it cooling and forming stars.
  • An object a few astronomical units across therefore regulates star formation across a galaxy a hundred thousand light years wide.
  • Galaxy formation models that leave this out predict far more massive galaxies than exist. The black hole is not a passenger — it is part of the mechanism.

06 Types

Three mass classes, and a suspicious gap

Black holes come in a range of masses spanning ten orders of magnitude, but not evenly. We know of many below a hundred solar masses and many above a hundred thousand, and remarkably few in between — a gap that is either a real feature of how they form or a limitation of how we look.

07 Formation

A core that runs out of anything left to burn

A star holds itself up by radiating. Fusion in the core supplies the outward pressure that balances its own weight, and each fuel it exhausts is replaced by a heavier one. Iron is where that chain ends: fusing iron consumes energy instead of releasing it. The support vanishes in less than a second.

  1. t − 10⁶ yrMain sequence

    Massive star

    Above roughly 20 M☉. It burns hydrogen ferociously and lasts only a few million years — the whole life of the star is shorter than the time since mammals appeared.

  2. t − 10⁵ yrShell burning

    Onion core

    The star swells into a supergiant. Inside, fusion runs in concentric shells — hydrogen outermost, then helium, carbon, neon, oxygen, silicon — each burning faster than the last.

  3. < 1 secondCollapse

    Core collapse

    The iron core exceeds 1.4 M☉ and electron pressure fails. An Earth-sized core falls to city size in about a quarter of a second, reaching a quarter of light speed.

  4. Hours to daysShock breakout

    Supernova

    The infall rebounds off the new core and blows the star apart, briefly outshining its entire galaxy. Some collapses fail instead and the star simply vanishes without a flash.

  5. t = 0Horizon forms

    Black hole

    If the remnant exceeds about 2.3 M☉, no known pressure can hold it. Collapse continues past neutron density, the horizon closes over it, and what is left is described by two numbers.

Fig 08 Timings for a star of roughly 25 M☉. The final stage is not a slow settling — the horizon forms during the collapse itself, in milliseconds.

Three other routes
  • Direct collapse. The most massive stars may skip the supernova entirely, falling straight through to a hole. Candidate disappearing stars have been observed.
  • Merger. Two holes spiral together and become one, radiating several solar masses of energy as gravitational waves. LIGO has now recorded well over a hundred of these.
  • Primordial. Density fluctuations in the first second after the Big Bang could have collapsed directly, producing holes of any mass. None has been found, and they remain a live dark-matter candidate.
The unsolved one
  • Quasars are observed at redshift 7 and beyond, already holding a billion solar masses less than 700 million years after the Big Bang.
  • Growing that from a stellar-mass seed by ordinary accretion takes longer than the universe had available. The arithmetic does not close.
  • Either the first seeds were already enormous — direct collapse of primordial gas clouds — or early accretion ran far above the rate we thought possible. We do not know which, and JWST was built partly to find out.

08 Tidal forces

Pulled apart by a difference, not a force

Gravity does not tear things apart. Gradients in gravity do. Falling feet-first, your feet are closer to the hole than your head and are pulled harder, and it is that difference — not the pull itself — that stretches you. The effect has an unhelpfully vivid name: spaghettification.

The inversion nobody expects

Because Δg at the horizon scales as 1/M², the bigger the black hole the gentler the crossing. A stellar-mass hole would pull you apart thousands of kilometres before you reached it. At a supermassive hole you would cross the horizon fully intact, feeling nothing at all, and only meet lethal tides much later on the way to the centre.

This is the opposite of the intuition, and it follows from geometry alone: doubling the mass doubles the horizon radius, and tidal force falls as the cube of distance. Size wins.

Seen from the outside
  • When a star wanders too close to a supermassive hole it is torn apart in a tidal disruption event. About half the debris is flung away; the rest forms a disk and lights up for months.
  • These are now caught several times a year by all-sky surveys, and they are the main way dormant black holes announce themselves.
  • The star does not fall in cleanly. It is stretched into a stream, wraps around the hole, and collides with itself — which is what produces the flare.

09 Gravitational time dilation

Clocks run slower lower down

Time is not a universal background. It runs at a rate that depends on where you are in a gravitational field, and near a black hole the effect stops being subtle. A clock at radius r ticks at √(1 − Rs/r) times the rate of a clock far away — and that factor goes to zero at the horizon.

You already live with this
  • Clocks on GPS satellites run about 45 microseconds per day faster than clocks on the ground, purely because they sit higher in Earth's gravity. Uncorrected, positions would drift by ten kilometres a day.
  • Your head ages very slightly faster than your feet. The effect is around one part in 10¹⁶ per metre — real, and measured in the laboratory.
  • Nothing about a black hole introduces new physics here. It is the same equation, evaluated somewhere steep.
The Miller's planet problem
  • Interstellar's first planet runs at one hour per seven Earth years — a factor of about 61,000.
  • For a non-rotating hole that requires orbiting at 1.0000000003 Rs, which is inside the innermost stable orbit. No such orbit exists.
  • Thorne's fix was to make Gargantua spin at very nearly the maximum allowed. Extreme rotation drags the innermost stable orbit down almost to the horizon, and a stable orbit with that dilation becomes genuinely possible.
  • The film's most implausible-sounding detail is the one that forced the most careful physics.

10 Against a neutron star

Almost the same size. Nothing else the same

Both are what a massive star leaves behind, and both are roughly the size of a city. The difference is whether anything stopped the collapse. A neutron star is held up — barely — by neutron degeneracy pressure. A black hole is what happens when that fails, and there is no next thing to try.

Fig 11 Drawn to one shared scale. A 2 M☉ neutron star is about 11 km in radius; a 3 M☉ black hole's horizon is 8.9 km. The gap between them — roughly 2.3 to 3 solar masses — is the narrowest and least-populated boundary in stellar astrophysics.

How the difference is actually measured
  • Look for the surface. Gas hitting a neutron star's crust decelerates and flashes in X-rays. Gas crossing a horizon simply stops being visible. The absence of that flash is positive evidence for a horizon.
  • Weigh it. Above roughly 2.3 M☉ no equation of state can hold a neutron star up. Anything heavier and compact is a black hole by elimination.
  • Listen to the merger. Gravitational waves from two neutron stars colliding carry a signature of matter being deformed. Two black holes merging produce a clean chirp and then silence, because there was nothing to deform.
  • GW170817 was a neutron-star merger and produced a visible flash of light across the electromagnetic spectrum. Black hole mergers produce no light at all, which is exactly what we observe.

What the EHT images actually are
  • Not photographs. Eight radio observatories on four continents observed simultaneously, and the Earth's rotation swept out an aperture the size of the planet. The image is reconstructed from interference patterns.
  • Not the black hole. The bright ring is hot gas; the dark centre is the shadow that gas is silhouetted against. The horizon is well inside the dark patch.
  • Resolution: around 20 microarcseconds — enough to read a newspaper in New York from a café in Paris.
  • The data volume was so large it was flown on hard drives rather than transmitted, and the South Pole disks had to wait for the Antarctic summer.
Why Sgr A* took three more years
  • Both shadows are about the same angular size, but Sgr A* is 1,500 times smaller, so its gas orbits 1,500 times faster.
  • M87*'s disk takes days to change. Sgr A*'s changes in minutes — the equivalent of photographing a running child with a long exposure.
  • The team had to model thousands of possible configurations and average them, which is why the published image is blurrier and took until 2022.

12 Misconceptions

Eight things that are almost right

Black holes attract bad explanations the way they attract gas. Most of these are not inventions — they are reasonable readings of a loose phrase, which is what makes them stick.

Black holes suck things in.

They pull exactly as hard as any other object of the same mass. Replace the Sun with a one-solar-mass black hole and every planet would continue on precisely the same orbit, in the dark. Nothing sucks; there is no inflow, no wind, no reaching out. You have to be aimed at one to fall in.

They will eventually eat their galaxy.

Sagittarius A* is 4.3 million solar masses in a galaxy of about 1.5 trillion — roughly three millionths of the total. It is also starved, accreting so little that it is millions of times fainter than its theoretical limit. Almost nothing ever gets close enough to fall in.

The Sun might become one.

It cannot. The Sun is about eight times too light to even reach the neutron-star route, let alone past it. It will become a red giant, shed its outer layers, and leave an Earth-sized white dwarf that cools quietly for the rest of time.

They are holes, or tunnels to somewhere else.

The word "hole" is doing real damage here. A black hole is a region, and a very dense one — there is more mass inside it than in most stars. Nothing is punctured and nothing leads anywhere. Wormholes are a separate mathematical solution with no observational support whatsoever.

Nothing can escape a black hole.

Nothing escapes from inside the horizon. Outside it, plenty does: jets carry away a substantial fraction of the infalling mass, and Hawking radiation means the hole itself slowly evaporates. A stellar-mass hole would take about 1067 years to do so — but the process is not zero.

You would be crushed the instant you crossed.

Depends entirely on the mass. At a stellar-mass hole you would be pulled apart thousands of kilometres out. At a supermassive one you would cross the horizon completely intact and notice nothing, because tidal forces at the horizon fall as 1/M². Bigger is gentler.

Time stops if you fall in.

Only as measured from outside. Your own watch keeps perfectly normal time all the way down, and you reach the centre in a short, finite interval. Distant observers see you freeze at the horizon; you see yourself cross it. Both descriptions are correct, and relativity does not prefer either.

We have photographed a black hole.

We have imaged the shadow that two of them cast on the gas behind and around them, using a virtual telescope the width of the Earth. The black hole itself is not in the picture and never can be. What the images prove is that the dark region is the right size and shape for a horizon — which is exactly the test that mattered.

The one that is still genuinely open
  • If a hole evaporates completely, what happened to the information about everything that fell in? Quantum mechanics says information cannot be destroyed. General relativity says it went somewhere unreachable and then the somewhere ceased to exist.
  • This is the information paradox, and it is not a puzzle about black holes. It is the sharpest known point of contradiction between the two theories physics is built on, which is why so much effort goes into a class of object we cannot visit.
  • Recent work on holographic entropy suggests information does come back out, encoded in the radiation. It is not settled.
