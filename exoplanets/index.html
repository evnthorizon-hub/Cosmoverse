01 Overview

A planet is a body that never managed to ignite

An exoplanet is any planet orbiting a star other than the Sun. That sounds like a definition about location, and the useful part of it is actually about mass: below roughly thirteen Jupiters nothing in a body's core will fuse, and it spends its whole existence cooling. Above eighty, hydrogen fuses and it is a star. Everything this page is about lives under the first of those two lines.

For most of the twentieth century the existence of other planetary systems was assumed rather than known. The assumption was reasonable and the evidence was nil: a planet is small, cold and sits next to something a billion times brighter, which is close to a worst case for observational astronomy.

The first confirmed planets outside our system turned up in 1992 around a pulsar — a dead star, detected by timing the arrival of its radio pulses to microsecond precision. The first around an ordinary star came three years later, and it was not what anyone had ordered: 51 Pegasi b is roughly half Jupiter's mass and orbits its star every four days, in a place where the accepted theory of planet formation said a giant planet could not exist.

That pattern has repeated ever since. Nearly every major result in the field has been a population nobody predicted, found by an instrument built to look for something else.

Four numbers worth holding on to
  • 6,366. Confirmed planets in the NASA Exoplanet Archive as of September 2026. The figure is genuinely provisional — the archive adds worlds most weeks, and occasionally removes one.
  • Zero. Exoplanets whose surface anyone has resolved. A few dozen have been imaged as single points of light. None is more than a point.
  • 84 parts per million. How much the Sun would dim if Earth crossed it from where you are sitting. That is the size of the signal the transit method is built to find.
  • Nine centimetres per second. How fast Earth makes the Sun wobble. Roughly walking pace, measured across light years, and still just out of reach.

02 Detection

Four ways to find a planet, and only one of them scales

Each method measures a different thing about the star and infers the planet from it. They are not interchangeable: two of them tell you a planet's size and mass between them, one of them is the only route to a spectrum, and one of them finds planets nobody will ever see again. Their shares of the catalogue differ by a factor of fifty.

01 / 04 Photometry

Transit

~75%

Watch a star continuously and wait for a planet to pass in front of it. The star dims by the fraction of its disk the planet covers — and that is a pure area ratio, so the depth of the dip is the planet's radius squared over the star's. It works only for the small fraction of systems we happen to view nearly edge-on, which is why it needs to stare at a hundred thousand stars at once to work at all. Kepler and TESS did exactly that, and between them they are why this method dominates.

Gives
Radius, period, inclination
Cannot
Mass — so no density without a second method
02 / 04 Spectroscopy

Radial velocity

~18%

A planet does not orbit its star; both orbit their shared centre of mass. So the star traces a small circle of its own, alternately approaching and receding from us, and its spectral lines shift blue then red on the orbital period. Measuring that shift is measuring a speed — metres per second, from a spectrum taken light years away. This is the method that found the first planet around a normal star, and it remains the only way to weigh most of them.

Minimum mass, period, eccentricity
Cannot
Radius; and mass stays a lower bound unless the inclination is known
03 / 04 Coronagraphy

Direct imaging

~1.5%

Physically block the star and photograph what is left. The difficulty is contrast: a young giant planet is perhaps ten thousand times fainter than its host, and an Earth in reflected light is ten billion times fainter. So this works, for now, only on planets that are massive, far from their star, and young enough to still be glowing with the heat of their own formation. It is also the only method that hands you the planet's own light, which makes it the only one with a route to a spectrum of a small world.

Gives
Actual photons, position, spectrum, orbital motion
Cannot
Reach old, small or close-in planets at all
04 / 04 Gravitational

Microlensing

~4%

When one star passes almost exactly in front of another, its gravity focuses the background star's light and the background star brightens for weeks. If the foreground star has a planet, the planet adds its own brief, sharp spike to that curve. No light from the planet is involved at all — only its mass. It is the one method sensitive to planets in wide orbits and to worlds bound to no star, and its great limitation is that the alignment never happens twice.

Mass and separation, including far-out and free-floating planets
Cannot
Ever be re-observed; the host is often unidentified

Why two methods are better than one
  • A transit gives you a size and nothing else. A dip of one per cent tells you the planet's disk is one per cent of the star's. It says nothing about what the planet is made of.
  • Radial velocity gives you a mass and nothing else. And strictly a minimum mass, because a shift along our line of sight is all we see — a heavy planet in a tilted orbit mimics a light one in an edge-on orbit.
  • Together they give a density. Which is the first number that says whether a world is rock, ice or gas. This is why the planets we understand best are the ones that both transit and wobble, and why a transiting planet round a bright, quiet star is worth a great deal more than one round a faint one.
  • A transit also gives an atmosphere. Because for a few hours the star's light is shining through the planet's air on its way to us. That is section 08.

03 Transit

The shape of a shadow, and what it gives away

A transit light curve has four pieces of information in it and each one is geometric. How deep the dip goes is the ratio of the two disks. How long it lasts is the planet's speed and the chord it cuts. How steep the sides are is the planet's own diameter. And how often it repeats is the orbital period, from which the distance follows.

The equation that does the work is the plainest one in astronomy. The star's brightness falls by the fraction of its disk the planet hides, so the depth of the dip is the square of the ratio of the radii and nothing else. No physics of the planet enters it — not mass, not composition, not temperature. Two areas.

That simplicity is also the method's ceiling. A transit tells you how big a planet is and refuses to tell you what it is. A dip of 0.6% could be a rocky world across a small star or a puffy gas planet across a large one, and photometry alone cannot separate them.

And it only works at all if we happen to be looking along the orbital plane. For an Earth-like orbit around a Sun-like star, the odds of that alignment are about one in two hundred. The other one hundred and ninety-nine systems are there and this method will never see them — which is worth remembering whenever a figure quotes how common planets are.

What each feature of the curve buys you
  • Depth → radius. Directly, given the star's radius, which comes from its spectrum and its distance.
  • Period → orbital distance. Kepler's third law, once the star's mass is known.
  • Ingress duration → the planet's diameter again, independently, as a cross-check on the depth.
  • Floor curvature → inclination. A planet crossing dead centre produces a different shape from one clipping the limb.
  • Timing drift → other planets. If transits arrive early and late on a cycle, something else in the system is tugging at the transiting planet. Dozens of worlds have been found this way without ever transiting themselves.

On a linear axis Earth's bar would be eight thousandths of Jupiter's and effectively invisible, which is the point: the same planet gives a signal seventy times larger in front of TRAPPIST-1 than in front of the Sun, purely because the star is a tenth the size. That is why the small-planet catalogue is overwhelmingly a catalogue of planets orbiting red dwarfs — not because they are commoner there, but because that is where we can see them. Kepler's photometric precision did reach the 84 ppm line; what defeated Earth analogues was the stars' own variability and the need to catch three transits of a year-long orbit.

04 Radial velocity

The star moves too, and you can hear it in the light

Nothing orbits anything else. Two bodies both orbit the point between them, and for a star with a planet that point lies just outside — or sometimes just inside — the star's own surface. So the star traces a small circle, alternately coming towards us and going away, and every line in its spectrum slides blue and then red on the orbital period. The whole measurement is a speed.

The quantity that comes out is called K, the semi-amplitude, and it depends on the planet's mass, the star's mass, the orbital distance and the angle we happen to be viewing the system from. Only the first of those is what we want, and the last of them is usually unknown — so what radial velocity delivers is strictly a minimum mass. A heavy planet in a face-on orbit produces the same tiny wobble as a light planet seen edge-on.

That ambiguity disappears the moment a planet also transits, because a transit only happens if the system is close to edge-on. This is why the handful of planets that both transit and wobble are worth far more than their number suggests: they are the only ones with both a radius and a real mass, and therefore the only ones with a density.

The method's frontier is now measured in centimetres per second. The best spectrographs working today reach something like ten to twenty-five centimetres per second on a quiet star. An Earth around a Sun would produce nine. It is close, and the obstacle has stopped being the instrument — the surfaces of stars boil, and that motion masquerades as a planet.

Why the frontier is no longer the instrument
  • Stars are not smooth. A photosphere is a boiling surface of convection cells, each rising and sinking at hundreds of metres per second. Averaged over the disk they mostly cancel — but not perfectly, and what is left moves at the same scale as the planet you are hunting.
  • Spots and plage make fake planets. A dark spot rotating across the disk removes blueshifted light from one limb and then redshifted light from the other, producing a periodic velocity signal at the star's rotation period. Several announced planets have turned out to be this.
  • So the work is now disentanglement. Modelling the star's own surface well enough to subtract it. That, rather than building a steadier spectrograph, is what stands between us and an Earth analogue by this method.

05 Types

Four kinds of world, and two of them we do not have

The Solar System has small rocky planets and large gas ones and nothing in between. The galaxy is not built like that. The two commonest kinds of planet we have found — super-Earths and mini-Neptunes — occupy exactly the size range our own system skipped, and the most abundant type of planet in the catalogue is one nobody had a name for in 1990.

01 / 04 8–15 R-Earth

Hot Jupiter

The first kind found

Lit by a G or K star

A gas giant orbiting closer than Mercury, often in under five days. Tidally locked, with a dayside between 1,000 and 2,400 K — hot enough to glow in visible light rather than merely reflect it. They cannot have formed where they are, so they must have migrated inwards, and working out how is still an open problem. They are also rare: fewer than one per cent of stars has one. They dominated the early catalogue only because they are the easiest thing in the sky to detect.

06 Habitable zone

A band where water could be liquid, and nothing more than that

The habitable zone is the range of distances at which a planet with an Earth-like atmosphere could hold liquid water on its surface. That is the whole claim. It is a statement about a star's output and an orbit, and it says nothing about whether a planet there has water, an atmosphere, a magnetic field, or a solid surface at all.

Three-quarters of all stars are red dwarfs, they live for hundreds of billions of years, and they are where nearly every small planet we know about is. On the face of it that is the best news in the field. The third row of the figure above is why it is complicated.

A planet that close to its star is almost certainly tidally locked, with one hemisphere in permanent day and the other in permanent night. Whether that is survivable depends entirely on whether it has enough atmosphere to move heat around the terminator — and young red dwarfs are violently active, throwing flares and coronal mass ejections that can erode an atmosphere over hundreds of millions of years. The two innermost TRAPPIST-1 planets have now been checked, and neither appears to have kept a thick one.

Which is why K dwarfs have become the interesting middle case. They live far longer than the Sun, they are far calmer than M dwarfs, and their habitable zones sit far enough out that a planet there need not be locked at all.

What the phrase does not mean
  • Not "has liquid water". Only that the distance permits it. Venus sits at the inner edge of the optimistic zone and has a surface at 464 °C.
  • Not "Earth-like". A gas giant can sit in a habitable zone, and several do.
  • Not fixed. Stars brighten as they age, so the zone migrates outward. Earth will leave it long before the Sun dies.
  • Not the only place water is liquid. Europa and Enceladus hold oceans far outside the Sun's zone, kept warm by tides rather than sunlight. The concept is a search filter for surface water, not a boundary on life.

08 Atmospheres

Reading the air by the light that got through it

During a transit, a thin ring of the planet's atmosphere is backlit by its own star. Molecules in that ring absorb at their own particular wavelengths, so at those wavelengths the planet blocks a little more light and appears very slightly larger. Measure the transit depth as a function of colour and the difference is a chemical inventory of a place nobody has been.

What has actually been detected, and what has not
  • Detected, repeatedly: water, carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, methane, sodium and potassium in the atmospheres of hot giant planets. Those are large, hot, puffy targets, and such measurements are now close to routine.
  • Detected, recently: carbon dioxide and signs of sulphur chemistry in warm Neptune-class planets. This is the current frontier, and every one of these is a multi-transit measurement with a hard-won signal.
  • Not detected: any atmosphere on an Earth-sized planet in a habitable zone. Several have now been examined carefully. The results are consistent with thin atmospheres, no atmospheres, or thick ones hidden beneath cloud — and telling those three apart is precisely the problem.
  • Not a biosignature. Nothing found so far requires life to explain it. Claims on this subject that reach the press have a poor record, and the reason is structural: the measurement is a few parts in ten thousand of a star's light, and the alternative explanations are numerous.

10 Timeline

Thirty-four years, and most of it in one decade

In 1990 the confirmed count was zero and the field did not exist. It now stands above six thousand. The shape of that curve is not gradual progress: it is two instruments. Kepler stared at one patch of sky for four years and roughly quadrupled the catalogue, and TESS has been surveying almost the whole sky since 2018.

12 Misconceptions

Eight things that sound right and are not

This field generates more misunderstanding than any other in astronomy, and very little of it is the public's fault. The words used in the literature — habitable, Earth-like, super-Earth — mean far narrower things than they appear to.

We have photographs of exoplanets.

About eighty have been directly imaged, and every one of them is a single unresolved point of light next to a much brighter one. Not one has been resolved as a disk, let alone a surface. Every picture of an exoplanet you have ever seen showing continents, oceans, clouds or rings is an artist's impression, including the four on this page — which is why each of them is labelled as one.

"Earth-like" means a planet like Earth.

In the literature it means a radius within roughly a quarter of Earth's, and sometimes an orbital distance. That is the entire content of the phrase. It implies nothing about atmosphere, surface water, plate tectonics, magnetic field, rotation, or whether the surface is solid — because for almost every planet so described, none of those has been measured.

A planet in the habitable zone is habitable.

The habitable zone is the range of orbital distances at which a planet with an Earth-like atmosphere could hold liquid water on its surface. It is a statement about a star's brightness and an orbit, and nothing else. Venus is inside the optimistic zone and has a surface at 464 °C under ninety atmospheres of carbon dioxide. Gas giants sit in habitable zones too.

Proxima Centauri b is the most promising world we know.

It is the nearest, which is not the same thing. It does not transit, so its radius has never been measured and its mass is only a lower bound. It orbits a flare star at a twentieth of an AU, almost certainly tidally locked, receiving periodic radiation doses that may have removed any atmosphere long ago. Its appeal is proximity, and proximity matters for future observation rather than for habitability.

Kepler found Earth's twin.

It did not, and one of the candidates announced in those terms no longer survives scrutiny — Kepler-452b was presented in 2015 as an Earth cousin around a Sun-like star, and later analysis concluded the signal is more likely a statistical artefact than a planet. Kepler's real achievement was statistical: it established how common small planets are, which is a more useful result than any single world.

The catalogue tells us what planetary systems are typically like.

It tells us what is detectable. Both leading methods are strongly biased towards large planets in short orbits around small or quiet stars, and the transit method additionally requires that we happen to be looking edge-on — which for an Earth-like orbit is about a one-in-two-hundred chance. Any statement about how common a kind of planet is has to be corrected for all of that first, and those corrections are where most of the difficult work in the field now lives.

During a transit we watch the planet cross the star.

We watch a number get smaller. The star is an unresolved point in the telescope and so is the planet; what is recorded is total brightness falling by a fraction of a per cent and then recovering. Every image of a transit, including Fig 03 on this page, is a reconstruction of a geometry inferred from that one falling number.

TRAPPIST-1 is seven Earths we might one day live on.

It is seven Earth-sized rocks orbiting a star at 2,566 K, all closer in than Mercury, all almost certainly tidally locked, and all well inside the flare radius of an active dwarf. JWST has now examined the two innermost and neither appears to retain a thick atmosphere. It is the most important system we know precisely because it is testable — not because it is hospitable.

Four that are true and sound false
  • The first exoplanets found orbit a corpse. Two planets around the pulsar PSR B1257+12, announced in 1992, detected by timing radio pulses from the collapsed remnant of a star that had already exploded.
  • Some planets have a year shorter than a working day. The shortest known orbital periods are close to four hours. The planet is skimming its star's surface and is being deformed by tides while it does it.
  • A planet's signal can be bigger around a smaller star. An Earth-sized world produces a transit seventy times deeper in front of TRAPPIST-1 than in front of the Sun. Smaller stars are better targets, not worse.
  • There may be more planets bound to no star than there are stars. Microlensing surveys keep finding objects with no detectable host. How many is genuinely uncertain, and it is one of the things the next generation of surveys is built to settle.
